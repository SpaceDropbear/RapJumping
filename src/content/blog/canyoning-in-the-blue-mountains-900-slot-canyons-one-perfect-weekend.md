Ask any Australian canyoner where they started, and there's a good chance the answer involves the Blue Mountains. West of Sydney, this UNESCO World Heritage-listed sandstone plateau country has been carved by millions of years of water erosion into an extraordinary density of slot canyons. Well over 900 of them have been identified and named, making the region Australia's undisputed canyoning heartland and one of the more significant canyoning destinations anywhere in the world for its sheer concentration of routes.

This guide covers what canyoning in the Blue Mountains actually involves, some of the region's best-known canyons across a range of difficulty levels, and the access and safety basics worth knowing before you plan a trip.

If you're canyon-curious but have never trusted a rope system before, a guided [rap jumping](/) session is a genuinely useful way to build confidence. It teaches abseiling technique in a fully supervised setting, before you tackle an abseil inside a genuine slot canyon.

## What makes Blue Mountains canyoning different

Blue Mountains canyons are formed where watercourses have cut narrow, often spectacular slots through the region's Narrabeen and Hawkesbury sandstone, frequently opening into wider, fern-lined valleys below. A typical descent combines walking, wading and swimming through pools, scrambling over boulders, sliding down water-polished rock chutes, and abseiling down waterfalls or drops too steep or slippery to downclimb: the exact mix depends entirely on which canyon you choose. Some canyons are short and largely non-technical; others involve multiple roped abseils, cold, deep swims through narrow, sunless slots, and genuinely serious commitment once you're in.

## Well-known canyons, from accessible to serious

The Blue Mountains' sheer number of canyons means there's a genuine range on offer, and choosing the right one for your group's fitness and experience matters more here than in almost any other adventure activity in this series:

- **Grand Canyon, Blackheath**: a scenic, more accessible canyon walk with attractive rock formations and glimpses of native wildlife, often used as an introduction to the region's canyon country.
- **Empress Canyon**: a genuine intermediate canyoning trip built around one of Australia's most popular single abseils, an approximately 30m drop at Empress Falls.
- **Serendipity Canyon, near Mount Wilson**: a fairly short, intermediate-level canyon with a handful of abseils and an exciting water jump, on the south side of the Wollangambe River.
- **Claustral Canyon**: part of the Carmarthen Labyrinth alongside Ranon and Thunder Canyons, and the most popular of the three; a genuine classic and a serious, committing multi-abseil descent for experienced canyoners.
- **Wollangambe Canyon 1 & 2, near Mount Wilson**: long, non-technical canyons (no abseiling required) that NSW National Parks describes as suitable only for fit canyoners and strong swimmers with good navigation skills, given their length and the 9km-plus return walk involved in Wollangambe 2.

That range (from a scenic Blackheath walk to a committing multi-abseil labyrinth) is exactly why "canyoning in the Blue Mountains" isn't a single, one-size-fits-all activity. Matching the canyon to your group's genuine fitness, swimming ability and canyoning experience is the single most important planning decision you'll make.

## Access, permits and current conditions

Independent, self-sufficient canyoning parties don't generally need a permit to canyon in Blue Mountains National Park. If you're booking a guided trip, the operator must hold a Parks Eco Pass, a licence issued by NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service to commercial tour operators working in NSW parks. It's a reasonable thing to check for before booking a guided canyoning trip. Conditions can also change quickly: NSW National Parks publishes local alerts for specific canyons covering closures, track damage and rockfall (a recent example affected the exit of Juggler/Point Pilcher Canyon), so checking current alerts for your specific canyon before you set out is worth the five minutes it takes.

## Safety and who Blue Mountains canyoning suits

Canyoning in the Blue Mountains demands more self-sufficiency than most adventure activities in this series: many canyons have no phone reception, limited or no rescue access partway through, and once you're committed to a descent, there's often no way out except to continue. Strong swimming ability, genuine fitness, cold-water tolerance and (for independent trips) solid navigation skills all matter, and flash flooding is a real risk if rain moves in while you're in a canyon. Beginners should start with a qualified, Parks Eco Pass-licensed guide rather than attempting an unfamiliar canyon independently: the gap between "looks like a nice walk" and "serious commitment once you're inside" is bigger here than almost anywhere else in Australian adventure sport. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many canyons are there in the Blue Mountains?

Well over 900 known slot canyons have been identified across the Blue Mountains region, making it Australia's canyoning heartland and one of the more significant canyoning destinations anywhere in the world for its density of routes.

### Do I need to be an experienced canyoner to try the Blue Mountains?

No, but choosing the right canyon matters. Some, like the Grand Canyon at Blackheath, are scenic and accessible; others, like Claustral Canyon, are serious, committing multi-abseil descents for experienced parties. Beginners should start with a qualified, Parks Eco Pass-licensed guide on an appropriate introductory canyon.

### Do I need a permit to go canyoning in the Blue Mountains?

Independent, self-sufficient parties generally don't need a permit. If you're booking a guided trip, the operator must hold a Parks Eco Pass, a licence issued by NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service to commercial tour operators.

### What's the most popular abseil in Blue Mountains canyoning?

The approximately 30m Empress Falls abseil, within Empress Canyon, is widely considered one of the most popular single abseils in Australian canyoning, and a genuine highlight for intermediate canyoners.

### Is canyoning in the Blue Mountains dangerous?

It can be, particularly for under-prepared parties. Many canyons have no phone reception or easy exit once you're committed, and flash flooding, cold water and strong swimming demands are real hazards. Checking current NPWS alerts and going with a qualified guide if you're inexperienced significantly reduces the risk.

### What's the best time of year to go canyoning in the Blue Mountains?

Late spring through to autumn generally offers more tolerable water temperatures. Some canyons remain genuinely cold year-round, so checking conditions and bringing appropriate wetsuit protection for your chosen canyon and season is worthwhile.
