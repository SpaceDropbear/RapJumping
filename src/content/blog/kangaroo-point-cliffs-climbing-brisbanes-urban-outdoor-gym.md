### TL;DR

- **Kangaroo Point Cliffs ("KP") are Brisbane's free, public outdoor crag** — a heritage-listed, quarried wall of volcanic Brisbane Tuff roughly 20 metres high on the southern bank of the Brisbane River, directly opposite the CBD and City Botanic Gardens.
- **They are floodlit in the evening**, so you can climb or abseil after work and into the night — a rarity for an outdoor venue and a huge part of KP's appeal during Brisbane's hot months.
- **Access is free and managed by Brisbane City Council** as part of Kangaroo Point Cliffs Park (River Terrace); a flat, quarried clifftop lined with large concrete bollards makes top-rope anchors simple and solid.
- **It suits every level**, but the rock is genuinely hard and polished in places — beginners should start on a top-rope, ideally with a qualified guide, and many people warm up to height with a guided abseil or rap jump first.
- **Bring sun sense and river-precinct manners**: KP is busy shared parkland with walkers and cyclists below, so set up considerately, never drop gear, and leave no trace.

If Australia had a textbook "urban crag", it would be Kangaroo Point. A short walk, ride or ferry hop from central Brisbane drops you at the base of a 20-metre cliff line that has trained generations of Queensland climbers. It is free, it is floodlit, and it is the easiest introduction to [rock climbing in Australia](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/) that any capital city can offer. This guide covers where it is, what the rock and the routes are actually like, the named walls and grade spread, how to get there, the floodlights, the safety realities of climbing in a busy public park, and the etiquette that keeps the place open to everyone.

## Where it is and who manages it

The cliffs sit below River Terrace in the inner-city suburb of Kangaroo Point, on the southern bank of the Brisbane River directly across from the City Botanic Gardens and the CBD. The skyline view from the top is one of the best in the city, which is exactly why the parkland above is a magnet for picnics, weddings and photographers as well as climbers.

The site is **heritage-listed** and forms part of **Brisbane City Council's Kangaroo Point Cliffs Park (River Terrace)**, a public reserve that runs along both an upper and a lower level. Climbing, bouldering and abseiling are recognised activities here and there is **no entry fee** — the park is open and publicly accessible. Council actively monitors and maintains the cliff face for public safety and to reduce the risk of rockfall, including a round of stabilisation works in 2024 with further maintenance planned, so it is worth checking for any current closures or works notices before a session.

Because it is shared parkland rather than a dedicated climbing reserve, courtesy is part of the deal: walkers, runners and cyclists pass directly below the wall, and the clifftop doubles as scenic public space. Climb like a guest and the venue stays welcoming.

## A quick history: the quarry that built Brisbane

Kangaroo Point is not a natural cliff — it is a quarry. From the convict era (roughly 1824–1841) onward, the distinctive volcanic rock here, known as **Brisbane Tuff**, was cut for building stone. Prized for its colour — pinks, greens, blue-greys and purples — the stone went into some of the city's earliest landmarks, including the Windmill (the Old Observatory) on Wickham Terrace, the Commissariat Store on William Street, the General Post Office on Queen Street, St Mary's Anglican Church at Kangaroo Point and the Cathedral of St Stephen.

Around 150 years of quarrying carved away the riverbank and left the sheer face climbers use today, with the quarry finally closing in the mid-1970s. The site was entered on the **Queensland Heritage Register on 28 February 2003**, listed as a quarry of state significance. That heritage status is part of why the wall is what it is: a clean, near-vertical, human-made face with a flat top — accidentally perfect for top-roping and abseiling.

## The rock and the climbing

KP is a quarried face of **Brisbane Tuff** — a hard, welded volcanic rock (the geology sits in the rhyolitic / ignimbrite family, often described loosely as rhyolite or tuff). It is mostly vertical, broken into bays by the old quarry lines, and stands around 20 metres at the climbed sections; the wider heritage rock face is described as reaching up to roughly 25 metres in places.

Two things define the climbing. First, because the wall was quarried, the **top is flat and lined with large concrete bollards** that make building top-rope anchors straightforward and bombproof — one of the main reasons KP is such a popular teaching venue. Second, the rock is **hard and, in the busy sections, polished**. Decades of traffic have buffed many of the holds smooth, so footwork, balance and trusting your feet matter far more than raw strength. It rewards good technique and punishes sloppy feet, which makes it an honest place to learn.

### Ways to climb it: top-rope, lead and abseil

KP supports the full spread of rope disciplines:

- **Top-roping** — the bread and butter here, and the right choice for beginners and anyone teaching. The rope runs from an anchor at the top, so a slip just means hanging on the rope. The bollard-lined clifftop makes anchor setup quick.
- **Lead climbing (sport and trad/mixed)** — there are bolted and natural lines for climbers with lead experience, who clip protection on the way up. Leading at KP is for people who already know how; it is not where you learn to lead unsupervised.
- **Abseiling and rap jumping** — the clean staging area and solid anchors also make KP a classic descent-skills venue. Abseiling lowers you down the rope facing the cliff; rap jumping is the forward-facing variant, where you walk or bound down the face looking out at the river and skyline. Both are popular first experiences for people who want the height and the exposure without having to climb up.

### The layout: named sections and where to start

Climbers have informally named the bays and walls along the cliff over the years, and the line is usually thought of as a series of distinct sections strung along the base path rather than one continuous wall. In practical terms, what you need to know is how the grades are spread, because that is what tells you where to go.

Grades in Australia use the open-ended **Ewbank scale**, a single number that starts around 1 (a walk-up) and climbs without an upper limit as routes get harder; Sport Climbing Australia is the national governing body for the sport. KP holds a genuinely wide spread:

- **Easy / beginner ground (around grade 8–14)** — lower-angle bays and gently featured walls. This is where top-rope sessions and first-timers belong, and where guided groups set up.
- **Mid-grade (around 15–20)** — steeper, more technical lines on that polished tuff; a solid bracket for improving climbers building footwork and endurance.
- **Hard test-pieces (21 and up)** — short, sharp and unforgiving, with the slick rock turning subtle moves into real cruxes.

Exact grades and names are best confirmed in a current local guidebook, and grades at any crag shift over time as holds wear or break. The point for planning is simple: there is friendly ground to learn on and hard ground to aspire to, all at one easy-to-reach wall.

## Climbing after dark: the floodlights

KP's signature feature is its lighting. The cliff precinct is lit in the evening, which means you can climb and abseil well after sunset — perfect for Brisbane's hot, humid months, when early mornings and nights are far more comfortable than the middle of the day. The floodlit wall against the city skyline is also simply one of the better atmospheres you will find at any urban crag.

Lighting is not on all night, and exact hours can change with the season and with any maintenance works. **Check Brisbane City Council's current park lighting hours before you plan a late session** rather than assuming, and have a head torch as backup. The same goes for any cliff stabilisation or closure notices.

## Getting there and parking

One of KP's biggest draws is access: it is genuinely in the middle of the city, and you do not need a car.

- **By ferry** — the Brisbane River is the obvious route. The **Holman Street** ferry terminal at Kangaroo Point (served by the Cross River ferry, connecting Holman Street, Riverside and Howard Smith Wharves) is a short walk from the cliffs precinct, and the **Maritime Museum** terminal at South Bank gives you access to the Cliffs Boardwalk along the base. CityCat and ferry services run frequently along the river.
- **By bus** — Kangaroo Point and the surrounding inner-south are well served by buses. Plan with TransLink's Journey Planner for current routes and times.
- **On foot and by bike** — the new **Kangaroo Point Bridge**, a dedicated pedestrian and cycle bridge connecting Kangaroo Point with the CBD, opened in December 2024 and makes walking or riding in from the city centre easy. The **Kangaroo Point Bikeway** and the riverside shared paths connect the precinct to South Bank and beyond. A steep staircase of 107 steps links the upper and lower levels of the cliffs.
- **By car** — parking is the catch. There is **metered parking** on River Terrace, Lower River Terrace and surrounding streets, plus some free non-metered street parking nearby, but it fills fast on fine evenings and weekends. Always check meters and signs. Given how good the public-transport access is, leaving the car at home is usually the smarter call.

## Facilities at the cliffs

Kangaroo Point Cliffs Park is a fully kitted-out riverside reserve, which makes a half-day session comfortable:

- **Public toilets** and water bubblers
- **Barbecues, picnic areas and shelters**, plus plenty of seating
- A **clifftop café** nearby
- **Bike racks** and a car park
- **Boat and canoe ramps**, a pontoon, table tennis and public art
- The **Cliffs Boardwalk** at the base and the Kangaroo Point Bikeway along the river

You will not find climbing-gear hire on site, so bring (or have your guide supply) everything you need.

## Is it good for beginners?

Yes — with the right setup. The easy access, flat top and solid anchors make KP an ideal first outdoor experience, but the rock itself is real and the consequences are real. The safest path for a first visit is a guided session or a structured [beginner pathway](/rock-climbing-for-beginners-in-australia-how-to-get-started/): you will be on a top-rope, supervised, with certified equipment and someone checking every knot and every belay.

If you are brand new to height and rope, a **guided abseil or rap jump with a qualified operator is a confidence-building first step** before you try to climb up. You get used to trusting the rope and the harness, to leaning back over an edge, and to the exposure — all the mental hurdles — without also having to figure out hard, polished moves. Plenty of people do a rap jump first, decide they love being on a cliff, and come back to climb.

## Staying safe in a busy public park

KP's biggest hazards are not exotic — they are the everyday ones, made sharper by the urban setting.

- **People below.** Walkers, runners and cyclists use the path directly under the wall. Anything you drop — a carabiner, a belay device, a water bottle, a rock you dislodge — can seriously hurt someone. Keep loose gear clipped or bagged, never throw equipment off the top, and call out if anything falls.
- **Anchors and rope wear.** The bollards are solid, but always inspect your anchor, pad sharp edges where the rope runs over the lip, and check knots and harnesses before you weight anything.
- **Sun and heat.** The wall catches sun and Brisbane gets hot and humid. Carry water, wear sunscreen and a hat between climbs, and favour mornings or floodlit evenings in summer.
- **The rock when wet.** Tuff is slick at the best of times and treacherous when damp. Give the wall time to dry after rain.
- **Rockfall and works.** Council monitors and maintains the cliff for stability, but loose rock can still occur on any natural-ish face. Helmets are sensible, watch what you pull on, and respect any maintenance or closure signage.

## Etiquette at the cliffs

A few unwritten rules keep KP pleasant and keep climbers welcome in shared parkland:

- **Keep the path and clifftop clear** — don't sprawl gear across the walkway or block the boardwalk.
- **Don't hog routes.** On a busy evening, share popular lines and don't leave top-ropes hanging on climbs you're not using.
- **Mind chalk and noise.** Brush off tick marks, keep music to yourself, and remember picnickers and weddings share the space.
- **Leave no trace.** Pack out everything, including tape and food scraps, and use the bins and toilets provided.

## Where to go next near Brisbane

Once you have found your feet at KP, the volcanic plugs of the [Glass House Mountains](/glass-house-mountains-rock-climbing-permits-routes-cultural-protocols/) offer a bigger, wilder day out north of the city, with their own access arrangements and cultural protocols to respect. For year-round training, wet-weather days and fast technique gains, the [best indoor gyms](/best-indoor-rock-climbing-gyms-in-australia-sydney-melbourne-brisbane-perth/) in Brisbane and beyond are the perfect complement to a session on real rock — and a great place to build the strength that polished tuff demands.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is climbing at Kangaroo Point free?

Yes. The cliffs are part of Brisbane City Council's Kangaroo Point Cliffs Park (River Terrace) and are publicly accessible at no charge for climbing, bouldering and abseiling. Guided sessions with operators cost money, but turning up with your own certified gear and competence does not.

### How high are the Kangaroo Point Cliffs?

The climbed wall is roughly 20 metres high, and Council describes abseiling down "the 20-metre cliffs"; the wider quarried heritage rock face is described as reaching up to about 25 metres in places. The flat, quarried top is lined with large concrete bollards that make solid top-rope and abseil anchors, which is part of why it is such a popular teaching crag.

### What grade are the climbs at Kangaroo Point?

KP holds a wide spread on the Australian Ewbank scale, from friendly beginner ground around grade 8 to 14, through technical mid-grades in the high teens, up to hard test-pieces of grade 21 and beyond. The rock is hard and polished in the busy sections, so footwork counts. Confirm specific routes and grades in a current local guidebook.

### Can you climb at Kangaroo Point at night?

Yes. The cliff precinct is floodlit in the evening, so night climbing is one of KP's best-known features and is ideal during Brisbane's hot months. Lighting is not on all night and hours can change, so check Brisbane City Council's current lighting hours before planning a late session and carry a head torch as backup.

### How do I get to Kangaroo Point Cliffs?

It is easy to reach without a car. Catch a ferry to the Holman Street terminal at Kangaroo Point or the Maritime Museum terminal at South Bank, take a bus, or walk and ride in via the Kangaroo Point Bridge and the riverside bikeway. Driving is possible but parking is limited to metered streets such as River Terrace and Lower River Terrace, so public transport is usually easier.

### Is Kangaroo Point suitable for abseiling and rap jumping?

Very much so. The clean, flat clifftop and solid bollard anchors make it a classic venue for descent skills, including abseiling and forward-facing rap jumping under qualified supervision. For many people, a guided abseil or rap jump here is the perfect first taste of being on a cliff before they try to climb up one.
