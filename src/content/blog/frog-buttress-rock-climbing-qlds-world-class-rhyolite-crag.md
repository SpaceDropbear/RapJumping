- **Frog Buttress is a rhyolite cliff on Mount French**, within Moogerah Peaks National Park near Boonah in South East Queensland, one of Australia's most historically important traditional ("trad") climbing areas, prized for its clean crack lines.

Ask a long-time Australian climber to name the handful of crags that shaped the sport in this country, and Frog Buttress will come up almost every time. Tucked into the rhyolite of Mount French, a couple of hours south-west of Brisbane, it is not a big cliff by world standards, but its columns of volcanic rock produced some of the cleanest, most sustained crack climbing in the country, and generations of Australian climbers cut their teeth here.

Today Frog Buttress sits within Moogerah Peaks National Park, managed by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service (QPWS), and remains a working, living trad area rather than a museum piece: climbers still rack up cams and wires and disappear into its corners and cracks every cooler-months weekend. It is also the natural starting point for a wider look at climbing across Queensland and Western Australia, which is why this article sits at the head of a growing series on crags and bouldering across both states.

This guide covers what Frog Buttress actually is, the style of climbing you will find there, how to get in via the national park system, when to go, and the gear and safety mindset trad climbing demands. Rock climbing shares its DNA with [rap jumping](/) and abseiling, all three depend on sound anchors, careful rope-work and a calm head at height, so if the idea of Frog Buttress's exposed rhyolite corners appeals but you are new to rope systems, a guided rap jumping session with a qualified operator is a controlled, well-supervised way to get comfortable on rope before you ever rack up gear on real trad terrain.

## About Frog Buttress: rock, history and character

Frog Buttress is a cliff on the north-western side of Mount French, within the Mount French section of Moogerah Peaks National Park, in the Scenic Rim local government area of South East Queensland, roughly 100km south-west of Brisbane near the town of Boonah. The park itself protects a cluster of rugged volcanic peaks, Mount French, Mount Edwards, Mount Greville and Mount Moon, that QPWS describes as significant refuges for rare and vulnerable wildlife, close to Brisbane yet still rugged and relatively undeveloped.

The rock is rhyolite, a fine-grained volcanic stone that at Frog Buttress has weathered into distinctive vertical columns. Where those columns meet, they form long, parallel-sided cracks, corners and aretes, exactly the kind of rock that rewards jamming technique and careful gear placement, and exactly why Frog Buttress became (and remains) a trad climber's crag rather than a sport-bolting project. QPWS's own management documentation for the park singles out the "trad" rock climbing experience at Frog Buttress as one of the park's key recreational values, describing it as internationally renowned as a premier traditional-style climbing location.

The history is well documented. Climbers Rick White and Chris Meadows are credited with pioneering rock climbing at Frog Buttress, establishing the first route in 1968. Within about a decade, upwards of 200 routes had been named on the cliff, and development has continued steadily since. QPWS's 2026 park management statement records that 380 routes have now been graded at Frog Buttress, with the range of established difficulty spanning a wide band of the Ewbank scale, from routes accessible to relative newcomers with a solid trad mentor, right through to routes reserved for genuine experts. Frog Buttress draws climbing visitors from well beyond Queensland, and its reputation among Australian trad climbers is longstanding.

Beyond the climbing, Mount French's exposed rhyolite summit has its own ecological significance: QPWS records that two species of lichen found on the summit occur nowhere else on Earth, part of the broader montane heath and shrubby woodland communities the park protects. That is worth knowing because it is part of why QPWS actively manages visitor impact around the cliff tops and access tracks, informal, visitor-made tracks and rope anchor points in these sensitive plant communities are a documented management concern.

## Climbing styles and grades at Frog Buttress

Frog Buttress is, first and foremost, a traditional climbing area. On a trad route, the leader places their own removable protection, spring-loaded camming devices, wired nuts (also called "stoppers") and slings, into the natural cracks and constrictions of the rock as they climb, and the second climber removes each piece on the way up. Nothing is left behind in the rock. This is a meaningfully different discipline from sport climbing, where routes are protected by permanently fixed bolts: trad demands that you can read the rock, judge gear placements under pressure, and build sound anchors, on top of the physical climbing itself.

QPWS is explicit that this is the intended character of the area: unauthorised bolting and the installation of unsanctioned anchor points at Frog Buttress is identified in the park's own management documentation as a direct threat to the trad climbing experience and, separately, to visitor safety, and it is something the park actively works to manage against. If you are used to sport crags, treat Frog Buttress as a different discipline entirely rather than "sport climbing without the bolts."

Grading follows the Ewbank system used across Australia and New Zealand, a single open-ended number rather than the letter-and-decimal systems used overseas, where the grade reflects the overall difficulty, sustainedness and protection quality of a route rather than just its hardest single move. Frog Buttress spans a genuinely wide range on this scale, from routes well within reach of a competent climber being mentored through their first trad leads, up through to grades that test the country's strongest climbers. Because we have not independently verified individual route names or specific grades for this article, we have deliberately avoided listing them here, check a current, area-specific route guide once you are planning an actual visit. For a full explanation of the Ewbank scale, how it compares with French and US grading, and what each grade band feels like in practice, see our companion guide, [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/), rather than have that table repeated here.

What the rock rewards, more than raw grade-chasing, is technique: hand and fist jamming, careful footwork on thin edges between the rhyolite columns, and the patience to place gear well rather than quickly. It is a style of climbing that builds genuinely transferable skills, many Australian climbers regard time spent at Frog Buttress as a formative part of learning to trust their own judgement on the rock.

## Getting there and access

Frog Buttress sits within Moogerah Peaks National Park (Mount French section), which is managed by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service, part of the Department of the Environment, Tourism, Science and Innovation. The park is around 100km south-west of Brisbane, and access is via the surrounding Scenic Rim road network to a car park at Mount French, from which walking tracks lead to the cliff base and the camping area.

There is a dedicated **Frog Buttress camping area** within the park, the only camping opportunity in this section of Moogerah Peaks, and it is walk-in only. Per QPWS's published information, it is a small area set in open eucalypt forest a short walk from the car park, with around 14 unnumbered sites, a maximum of 28 people permitted in the camping area at a time, picnic tables, and toilets nearby. Open fires are prohibited (fuel stoves only), generators are not allowed, and campers need to carry in their own gear, including drinking water. Camping permits must be booked and paid for in advance through the Queensland Government's online camping booking system, and standard Queensland national park camping fees apply. QPWS notes that vehicle parking at the car park is limited during high-visitation periods such as the climbing season and school holidays, so plan to arrive early or coordinate with other climbers.

Because this is a live, managed natural area, always check current conditions before you travel:

- QPWS park alerts for Moogerah Peaks National Park, for any temporary closures, track works or fire-related restrictions.
- Bureau of Meteorology forecasts and current bushfire warnings for the Scenic Rim area.
- Your camping permit is booked in advance if you intend to stay overnight, walk-up camping is not guaranteed.
- Boot-cleaning and biosecurity signage at the car park and track head. Phytophthora cinnamomi (a plant pathogen that causes root rot, sometimes called dieback) has previously been detected in the park, and QPWS maintains boot scrub stations to help stop its spread between sites, please use them.

The Australian Climbing Association Queensland (ACAQ) is the recognised peak body and consultative entity for climbing access matters in the state, and works with QPWS and other land managers on issues affecting crags like Frog Buttress. Supporting or joining your state's Australian Climbing Association is one of the most practical things a climber can do to help keep areas like this open for the long term.

## Best season and conditions

Frog Buttress is a cooler-months crag. QPWS notes that the Frog Buttress camping area is heavily used by the climbing community from Easter through to late October, broadly, autumn, winter and spring, which lines up with when the exposed rhyolite is most comfortable to climb on. Queensland's subtropical summer brings heat and humidity that make the north-west-facing buttress considerably less pleasant, and afternoon storms are more common over summer in this part of the Scenic Rim.

Expect the camping area and car park to be at their busiest on autumn and spring weekends, over school holidays and around Easter, when parking is genuinely limited. Winter weekdays tend to be quieter. As with any outdoor crag, avoid climbing on wet rock, both because rhyolite crack lines lose friction when damp and because climbing on saturated rock and vegetation causes more erosion damage, one of the specific visitor-impact concerns QPWS has documented for this park.

## Gear and safety at Frog Buttress

Trad climbing is technically demanding and carries meaningfully higher risk than climbing on fixed, inspected bolts. Your protection is only as good as your own placement, judged in the moment, under whatever pressure the route is putting on you, which is precisely why trad is not a discipline to self-teach from books or videos. If you are new to placing gear, the safest and fastest way to build real competency is through mentorship from an experienced trad climber or structured instruction from a qualified guide, ideally over multiple sessions rather than a single day.

Typical trad gear for a crag like Frog Buttress includes:

- **Camming devices ("cams")** in a range of sizes, to protect parallel-sided cracks, exactly the kind of crack systems rhyolite columns produce.
- **Wired nuts / stoppers**, for constrictions and tapering cracks, generally lighter and cheaper than cams but requiring good placement judgement.
- **Slings and extendable quickdraws**, to reduce rope drag as the rope zig-zags between placements up a crack line.
- **A double or single dynamic rope** suited to the route length and your protection strategy.

As with any climbing hardware used in Australia, look for recognised certification: carabiners to EN 12275 (and/or the equivalent UIAA standard), harnesses to EN 12277, dynamic ropes to EN 892, and helmets to EN 12492. A helmet is particularly important on a trad crag, where rockfall risk and the consequences of an unexpected fall are generally higher than on a well-bolted sport wall. Our [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/) covers these standards, and general gear and safety fundamentals, in more depth.

Finally, respect the area's specific management concerns: do not install bolts or new anchor points (this is contrary to the trad ethic of the area and is actively managed against by QPWS), stick to formed tracks rather than cutting new routes through the sensitive heath vegetation around the cliff tops, use the boot scrub stations to help prevent the spread of plant disease, and pack out everything you bring in, including toilet waste where facilities are not available.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is Frog Buttress and who manages it?

Frog Buttress is a rhyolite cliff on Mount French, within Moogerah Peaks National Park near Boonah in South East Queensland, roughly 100km south-west of Brisbane. It is managed by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service (QPWS), part of the Department of the Environment, Tourism, Science and Innovation.

### Is Frog Buttress a sport climbing or trad climbing area?

Frog Buttress is a traditional ("trad") climbing area. Climbers place their own removable protection, cams, wired nuts and slings, rather than clipping fixed bolts, and QPWS's own park management documentation identifies unauthorised bolting as a threat to the area's trad climbing character.

### How many routes are there at Frog Buttress and how are they graded?

We have not listed individual route names or specific grades in this article; check a current route guide when planning an actual visit.

### Can I camp at Frog Buttress?

Yes. There is a dedicated walk-in Frog Buttress camping area within Moogerah Peaks National Park, with around 14 unnumbered sites and a maximum of 28 people. It has picnic tables and nearby toilets, prohibits open fires (fuel stoves only) and does not allow generators. Camping permits must be booked in advance online and standard Queensland national park camping fees apply.

### When is the best time to climb at Frog Buttress?

Cooler months. QPWS notes the camping area is heavily used by the climbing community from Easter through to late October, broadly covering autumn, winter and spring. Queensland's hot, humid summer makes the exposed rhyolite considerably less comfortable to climb.

### Is Frog Buttress suitable for someone new to trad climbing?

Only with proper mentorship or qualified instruction. Trad climbing requires the skill to place your own protection safely, which carries real technical risk if self-taught. Newcomers should climb with an experienced trad partner or a qualified guide, and build skills progressively rather than attempting to lead unsupervised.

## New to climbing? Build rope confidence first

Frog Buttress represents trad climbing at a genuinely serious level, the kind of crag experienced climbers spend years working towards. If that world appeals but you have never clipped into a harness or trusted a rope system at height, the smartest first step is not a national park crag; it is a guided, fully supervised introduction to rope work. A guided rap jumping (forward-facing abseiling) or abseiling session with a qualified operator, equipment supplied and every system checked by an instructor, gives you a genuine, controlled taste of exposure, height and trusting your gear: the same fundamentals that underpin everything a trad climber does at a crag like Frog Buttress. It is an excellent, low-commitment way to find out whether the vertical world is for you before you invest in gear, courses and mentorship, and when you are ready for the bigger picture, our [Rock Climbing in Australia hub](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/) maps out the styles, grades and pathways from there.
