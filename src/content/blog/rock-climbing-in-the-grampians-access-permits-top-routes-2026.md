The Grampians — **Gariwerd** to its Traditional Owners — is, alongside nearby Mt Arapiles, the beating heart of Victorian climbing. Picture the overhanging orange perfection of Taipan Wall glowing in the late sun, the sweeping multi-pitch faces of Mt Rosea, and a galaxy of world-famous boulder problems tucked among the blocks below Hollow Mountain. It is spectacular climbing. It is also a profoundly important cultural landscape, which is exactly why access here works differently from almost anywhere else in Australia. This guide explains how to climb the Grampians in 2026 the right way: where the climbing is, how the designated-area and permit system works, which walls have recent closures, and how to plan a safe, respectful trip. For the wider national picture, start with our hub on [rock climbing in Australia](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/).

## Where it is, and why it is special

Gariwerd/Grampians National Park sits about three hours' drive west of Melbourne, near the town of Halls Gap, in the Wimmera region of western Victoria. It is a large park — a series of rugged sandstone ranges running roughly north to south — and the climbing is spread across several distinct precincts, from the northern Mt Stapylton and Hollow Mountain area down to the central crags near Halls Gap and the southern and western ranges.

The rock is predominantly sandstone, but it is far from uniform. In the north you find steep, featured, often overhanging walls with bullet-hard orange streaks; further south there are huge, broken faces that lend themselves to long multi-pitch adventures, plus shorter, fierce crags with roofs and powerful corners. That variety — easy classics in beautiful settings sitting beside some of the hardest routes in the country — is a big part of what makes Gariwerd special. So is its cultural significance: the landscape holds the richest concentration of Aboriginal rock art in Victoria, and that fact shapes everything about how and where you are allowed to climb.

## Access in 2026: designated areas and the new permit

This is the part to get right before anything else. Following the **Greater Gariwerd Landscape Management Plan** (released in 2021 by Parks Victoria in partnership with the Traditional Owners, represented by the Barengi Gadjin, Eastern Maar and Gunditj Mirring Aboriginal corporations), climbing in Gariwerd is now managed under a clear, deliberate framework.

### Designated areas only

Parks Victoria has identified **122 designated rock-climbing areas**, including 13 bouldering areas. **Climbing is not permitted beyond these areas.** This is the single most important rule in the park. The areas were defined through extensive assessment in partnership with Traditional Owners and climbing representatives, and the list has grown over time — in May 2025, Parks Victoria reopened 18 reviewed priority areas, bringing the total to 122. More priority areas may be assessed in future, but Parks Victoria has stated that **no further bouldering areas will be opened**, owing to the impacts of the activity.

A crucial subtlety: the designated areas are *areas*, not individual routes. Within any one designated area there can be many separately named climbs, and an area being "open" does not mean every line within or near it is open. It is your responsibility to confirm that the specific route you want to climb actually sits inside a current designated area. On-ground signage is being rolled out, but with 122 areas you cannot rely on signs alone — carry the official maps and the designated-areas list.

### The free online permit

Parks Victoria is developing a **rock-climbing permit that will be free and completed online**. Once established, it will be **mandatory for everyone** who wishes to climb in Grampians (Gariwerd) National Park. Parks Victoria has indicated there is still logistical work to finalise and that implementation could take up to a year, so at the time of writing it may not yet be live. Beyond access control, the permit is intended to become the main channel through which Parks Victoria keeps climbers up to date about changing conditions, new areas opening and closures — so registering will be both a requirement and a useful way to stay informed. Check the Parks Victoria "Rock climbing in Gariwerd" page for the current status and register before your trip if it is active.

### Conditions every climber agrees to

Within the designated areas, Parks Victoria asks climbers to follow a short, important set of conditions:

- Climb **only** in designated climbing areas.
- Accept that you are responsible for assessing and managing the risks of the activity.
- **Minimise chalk**, and use only chalk coloured to match the rock surface.
- **Adhere to all signs and notifications**, and never enter a closed area.

Fixed protection (bolts) may be used and installed within designated areas to enable climbing, but **not** within the buffer zones that some areas contain to protect cultural or environmental values. When in doubt, treat the official information as the only source of truth — guidebooks, apps and crag websites are handy for finding routes, but they may not reflect the current legal access status, and they are not the authority.

## Current closures and access notes (check before you go)

Access in Gariwerd is genuinely dynamic: when Aboriginal cultural heritage is identified or rediscovered, areas can be closed at short notice while Parks Victoria and Traditional Owners work out a longer-term response. Treat everything in this section as a snapshot, and confirm the live status on the Parks Victoria page before you plan around any wall.

- **Taipan Wall (Gunigalg):** a section of the wall is open. Following discussions with Traditional Owners and the Gariwerd Wimmera Reconciliation Network, alternate access points were agreed to reach the northern section; as a result some climbs have been rerouted and some are now closed. Read the official Taipan Wall information before climbing there.
- **Spurt Wall:** not within the Taipan Wall (Gunigalg) designated area, and significant cultural values have been identified — it is not open to climbing. On-ground signage marks where climbing is and is not permitted.
- **Centurion Walls:** a partial closure is in place after significant cultural values were rediscovered at Lower Centurion in late 2025. Access is closed from the area north of and including the route Damage Done; climbs south of and including In Plain Sight remain open; and access to Upper Centurion is unchanged. Read the official Centurion Walls information first.
- **Tortoise Wall:** a temporary closure is in place to protect Aboriginal cultural heritage rediscovered in April 2026, while a longer-term management response is determined. Visitors are asked not to access the area.
- **Mountain Lion:** visitors are asked not to access this area following cultural heritage rediscovered in December 2023, pending a full assessment.
- **Summerday Valley:** to protect cultural values, you can climb here only with a Licensed Tour Operator — it is not open to independent climbers. It is also where Parks Victoria provides all-abilities climbing.

One more piece of recent context worth knowing: during the summer of 2024–25, widespread bushfires impacted more than 135,000 hectares across the central, west and south-eastern sections of the park, damaging campgrounds, walking tracks and many designated climbing areas. Parks Victoria has been running a phased recovery program and many sites have reopened, but it is well worth checking the park's "change of conditions" notices for any fire-related closures alongside the cultural ones.

## The precincts: where the climbing is

Below is an orientation to the main climbing precincts and what each is known for. Grades are quoted on the Australian Ewbank scale. Remember that within every precinct, what is actually open is governed by the current designated-area list and any active closures — so use this as a map of the terrain, not a guarantee of access.

### Northern Grampians — Mt Stapylton and the Amphitheatre

The northern end, centred on Mt Stapylton, is the most developed and most visited part of the Grampians, and for many climbers it is the main event. Its crown jewel is **Taipan Wall**, a long, steeply overhanging sweep of orange sandstone widely regarded as one of the finest single crags in Australia — and, by reputation, in the world. The hard, sustained sport lines here are a bucket-list objective for strong climbers, with the access caveats noted above. Around the broader Stapylton and Amphitheatre area there is also adventurous trad and multi-pitch climbing on big, exposed faces for experienced parties with the right rack and head for height.

### Northern Grampians — Hollow Mountain and Andersens (bouldering)

The northern Grampians is also a world-class bouldering destination. The Hollow Mountain area — including the famous Hollow Mountain Cave and the nearby Andersens boulders — is one of the great Australian bouldering venues, with concentrated, often powerful, overhanging problems among the sandstone blocks. Thirteen of the designated areas are specifically for bouldering, and (as above) no new bouldering areas will be added, so it is especially important here to keep to the designated blocks and the agreed access. Trackside bouldering in this northern area was where modern Grampians bouldering really took off.

### Central Grampians — Mt Rosea and the Halls Gap crags

Closer to Halls Gap, the central ranges offer a different flavour. **Mt Rosea** is the Grampians' biggest cliff and a magnet for long, classic multi-pitch trad routes, with grades extending into the low-to-mid 20s (Ewbank) — sustained, position-rich climbing that rewards solid gear skills and route-finding. The wider Halls Gap precinct holds a spread of crags across the full grade range and is the most convenient base for many visitors.

### Bundaleer and the southern/western ranges

**Bundaleer** is a shorter but imposing crag known for powerful lines, steep corners and beetling roofs; many of the easier routes are trad, while a lot of the harder lines mix gear and bolts. It has a reputation as a summer crag thanks to its aspect and shade. Further afield, the southern and western ranges (including areas such as the Victoria Range and the Serra Range) hold additional designated areas with their own character — often quieter, more remote, and demanding genuine self-sufficiency. Always cross-check these against the current designated-area maps, as access and fire recovery vary.

## Grades and who the Grampians suits

The Grampians spans the full Australian Ewbank range, from gentle slabs to some of the hardest routes in the country, but its overall reputation is for climbing that is steep, committing and physical. It rewards climbers who already have solid technique and fitness and, for the trad and multi-pitch terrain, sound anchor-building, gear-placement and route-finding skills. Strong sport climbers come here for the marquee hard lines; trad climbers come for the long adventures on Rosea and the fierce single-pitch test-pieces at Bundaleer.

Newer climbers are not shut out, but the smart path is to build foundations first — indoors and on friendlier, more moderate crags — before tackling Gariwerd's steeper, more serious walls. Our [Blue Mountains guide](/blue-mountains-rock-climbing-crags-grades-guided-tours/) is a good reference for more moderate sandstone with established guided options, and it is worth reading our [essential gear guide](/essential-rock-climbing-gear-guide-for-australian-climbers/) before you invest in a rack for trad and multi-pitch days. If you are brand new and just want a taste of height and exposure in expert hands, a guided session (or the all-abilities, Licensed-Tour-Operator-only climbing in Summerday Valley) is the way to start.

## Respecting Country

Gariwerd has been living, cultivating, ceremonial and dreaming Country for Traditional Owners for more than 22,000 years. The landscape contains more than 500 registered Aboriginal places — hundreds of them within the park itself — and the richest concentration of Aboriginal rock art in Victoria. Climbing here is not a right; it is a shared arrangement, agreed through the Greater Gariwerd Landscape Management Plan and depending entirely on climbers doing the right thing.

In practice that means: climb only within current designated areas; never climb on, near or over rock art or other cultural sites; minimise chalk and colour-match it to the rock; keep groups small; carry out everything you bring in; and follow Parks Victoria's signs, notifications and closures without exception — including the requests not to access areas such as Tortoise Wall and Mountain Lion. When a closure appears, the respectful response is simply to go elsewhere. Access lost through carelessness or a sense of entitlement is extremely hard to win back, and it affects every climber who comes after you. Treating Gariwerd as the cultural landscape it is, first and foremost, is the foundation of climbing there at all.

## Seasons, weather and fire risk

Autumn and spring are the prime climbing seasons in the Grampians, offering cool, generally stable conditions that suit both the long days needed for multi-pitch routes and the friction required for hard sport and bouldering. Winter brings crisp, short days with good climbing on sunny, sheltered aspects, though some shaded and high routes can be cold and damp. Summer is the season to approach with the most caution: it can be very hot, and western Victoria carries serious bushfire risk.

Before any summer trip — and on any hot, windy day — check the fire danger rating and whether a **total fire ban** is declared for the Wimmera district. On declared total-fire-ban days, and during periods of extreme or catastrophic fire danger, the safest and most responsible choice is often to stay out of the ranges entirely. Conditions can change fast, access roads can close, and the remoteness that makes the climbing special also makes a fire situation far more dangerous. Build flexibility into your plans and never push into the park on a high-risk day just to tick a route.

## Base towns, logistics and self-sufficiency

Most climbers base themselves at **Halls Gap**, the main town inside the ranges, which has accommodation, food, fuel and the Brambuk the National Park and Cultural Centre — a good place to ask Parks Victoria staff about current climbing conditions. The other classic base is **Natimuk**, a small town on the western side that has long been the heart of the local climbing community and, importantly, sits right next to Mt Arapiles.

Wherever you stay, treat the Grampians as a remote-area trip. Many designated areas involve a drive on unsealed roads followed by a bushwalk in, with **no facilities and limited or no mobile reception** at the crag. Come self-sufficient: carry plenty of water, food, sun protection and a first-aid kit; take a paper map and a means of navigation that does not rely on phone signal; tell someone your plans and expected return; and consider a personal locator beacon for the more remote areas. Download the official Parks Victoria maps and designated-areas list before you lose reception, and double-check closures while you still have signal.

## Pair it with Arapiles

Most climbers who make the trip west also climb at nearby [Mt Arapiles (Dyurrite)](/climbing-at-mt-arapiles-australias-trad-climbing-mecca/), roughly an hour from the northern Grampians. The two together are the classic Victorian climbing road trip: the Grampians for steep sport, big multi-pitch lines and world-class bouldering; Arapiles for its dense concentration of superb, well-protected trad. Arapiles has its own evolving access and cultural-heritage considerations, so as with Gariwerd, check the current status before you go and climb respectfully.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you still rock climb in the Grampians?

Yes — within the rules. Parks Victoria permits climbing in 122 designated areas across Gariwerd (Grampians) National Park, including 13 bouldering areas, and climbing outside those areas is not allowed. Some walls have temporary or partial closures to protect Aboriginal cultural heritage, so always check the current status on the Parks Victoria "Rock climbing in Gariwerd" page before you go.

### Do I need a permit to climb in the Grampians?

A free online climbing permit for Gariwerd is being introduced by Parks Victoria. Once it goes live it will be mandatory for everyone who wishes to climb there, though implementation may take some time, so check whether it is active and register before your visit. The permit will also be used to keep climbers updated about changing conditions and closures.

### Why are some Grampians climbing areas closed?

Closures protect Aboriginal cultural heritage. The respectful response is to keep out of any closed or restricted area and climb elsewhere.

### Is the Grampians good for beginners?

It is better suited to climbers with some experience, as the rock tends to be steep and committing. Beginners are best off learning indoors or on more moderate crags, or booking a guided session, before tackling the Grampians. There is also all-abilities climbing in Summerday Valley, which is accessible only with a Licensed Tour Operator.

### When is the best time to climb in the Grampians?
