- **You can climb and abseil on a small number of peaks — but not all of them.** Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service (QPWS) lists Mount Ngungun (253 m), Mount Tibrogargan (364 m) and Mount Beerwah (556 m) as roped-sport locations for experienced, well-equipped climbers and those under their direct supervision.
- **Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck) is closed.** It has been a Restricted Access Area under the *Nature Conservation Act 1992* since March 1999 because of a high to very high rockfall risk. On-the-spot fines apply — do not climb it.

- **This is serious, exposed terrain — serious injuries and deaths have occurred.** The summit routes are not walking tracks; helmets, real climbing equipment and rock-scrambling experience are essential, and you should never climb in the wet.
- **Always check current closures with QPWS before you go.** Conditions, alerts and access can change. Start at the official Glass House Mountains National Park park-alerts and "Things to do" pages.

The Glass House Mountains are one of the most arresting landscapes in South East Queensland — a cluster of jagged volcanic plugs that erupt from the flat coastal plain of the Sunshine Coast hinterland, roughly 70 km (about an hour's drive) north of Brisbane off the Bruce Highway. For climbers and abseilers they are equal parts iconic and intimidating: steep, often friable rock, real exposure, and a managed-access regime that you genuinely need to understand before you load a pack. For the wider national picture, this guide sits alongside our hub on [rock climbing in Australia](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/).

It is also Country of profound cultural significance. The peaks are sacred to the Jinibara and Kabi Kabi (Gubbi Gubbi) peoples, and that significance shapes how the park is managed and how responsible visitors behave. This article covers which peaks you can actually climb and abseil under current QPWS guidance, which are off-limits, the very real hazards, the best season, the practicalities of getting there — and how to respect the people and the place. It is a planning overview, not a how-to: climbing and abseiling these peaks safely demands qualified instruction and experience. If you are new to ropes, the right first step is supervised instruction — see our [beginner's pathway for Australia](/rock-climbing-for-beginners-in-australia-how-to-get-started/) — rather than turning up to a remote volcanic plug to work it out as you go.

## Where the Glass House Mountains are

The Glass House Mountains National Park lies in the Sunshine Coast hinterland, about 70 km north of Brisbane — close to an hour by car via the Bruce Highway and local roads. The townships of Beerburrum and Glass House Mountains sit at the heart of the area and are your orientation points for trailheads. The peaks themselves are remnants of volcanic activity from roughly 25–27 million years ago: molten rock that solidified into hard trachyte and rhyolite, then was exposed over millions of years as the softer surrounding rock eroded away. The dramatic vertical columns you can see on Mount Beerwah and Mount Ngungun formed as that volcanic rock cooled.

That geology is exactly why the climbing is what it is — sheer faces, columns and slabs — and why loose, friable rock is such a persistent hazard. These are not bombproof granite domes; QPWS explicitly describes sections of the popular summit routes as having very loose, unstable rock.

## Which Glass House Mountains can you climb or abseil?

This is the single most important thing to get right, because the peaks are *not* uniformly open. QPWS lists specific locations as suitable for roped sports — and separately maintains a long-standing legal closure on one peak. The table below summarises the current status as published by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service. Heights and the climbing/abseiling status come from official QPWS pages; always confirm against current park alerts before you travel.

| Peak | Height (above sea level) | Climbing / abseiling status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mount Ngungun** | 253 m | Listed by QPWS for roped sports — moderately challenging rock-face climbing and abseiling (about 20–40 m pitches). | Also has a popular Grade 4 summit walking track (2.8 km return, allow 2 hr). Equipment and expertise essential for any roped activity. |
| **Mount Tibrogargan** | 364 m | Listed by QPWS for roped sports — a challenging and potentially dangerous rock-face climbing site. Also a scrambling summit route. | The main rock-climbing and scrambling peak. First section of the summit route has very loose, unstable rock; "No waiting zone" is a high-risk rockfall area. High level of expertise and equipment required. |
| **Mount Beerwah** | 556 m (the highest peak) | Listed by QPWS for roped sports — challenging and potentially dangerous climbing; a steep summit route also exists. | Culturally very significant. High-risk rockfall zones along the route. Confirm current access and any closures with QPWS before attempting. |
| **Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck)** | 377 m | **Closed.** Restricted Access Area under the *Nature Conservation Act 1992* since March 1999. No public access; climbing is not permitted. | High to very high rockfall risk per the 1999 geological report. On-the-spot fines apply (3 penalty units; maximum 80). Do not enter or climb. |
| **Mount Beerburrum** | 280 m | Not listed as a roped-sport climbing location; it has a steep, paved summit *walking* track only. | Grade 3 walk (3.5 km return, allow 1 hr 30 min) to a fire tower. A good vantage point, not a climbing objective. |

In short: under current QPWS guidance the roped-sport peaks are **Mount Ngungun, Mount Tibrogargan and Mount Beerwah**, all strictly for experienced, well-equipped parties (or people under direct expert supervision). **Mount Coonowrin is off-limits.** Because alerts and closures can change at any time, treat the QPWS park-alerts page as the source of truth on the day you visit, and never assume a peak or route is open.

## The peaks in detail

### Mount Tibrogargan — the main climbing and scrambling peak

Tibrogargan (364 m) is the peak most people mean when they talk about "climbing the Glass House Mountains". The base of the mountain is encircled by the family-friendly Tibrogargan circuit (4.1 km return, Grade 3), but the summit route is a different proposition entirely. It begins about 1 km along the circuit, just past the Mountain View lookout, and QPWS describes the first section as having very loose and unstable rock until you reach a marked "No waiting zone" — a high-risk rockfall area where you should not linger. From there the route climbs a near-vertical rockface to the top. QPWS likens the effort to ascending a 76-storey building and stresses it requires high fitness and genuine rock-scrambling skill. For roped climbing and abseiling, this is also a recognised site, but expressly for those with a high level of expertise and equipment. Peregrine falcons are often seen soaring around Tibrogargan, a reminder that the cliffs are living habitat, not just rock.

### Mount Beerwah — the highest, and deeply sacred

Beerwah (556 m) is the tallest of the Glass House Mountains and, in Jinibara and Gubbi Gubbi tradition, is understood as the mother of the range — Country of the deepest cultural importance. QPWS lists Beerwah among the roped-sport locations and publishes a summit route (allow 3–4 hours, with high-risk rockfall zones and a "No waiting zone"), describing the climb as equivalent to ascending a 111-storey building. It is steep, committing and unforgiving of error. Given both the physical seriousness and the profound cultural significance, this is a peak to approach with particular humility: confirm current access with QPWS, weigh the request from Traditional Owners discussed below, and never treat it as a casual tick.

### Mount Ngungun — the most accessible introduction to the rock

Ngungun (253 m) is the gentlest of the three roped-sport peaks and the one most visitors meet first, usually via its summit walking track (2.8 km return, Grade 4, allow 2 hours) that ends with sweeping views to Tibrogargan, Coonowrin and Beerwah. For roped sports, QPWS describes it as offering moderately challenging rock-face climbing and abseiling on pitches of roughly 20–40 m. The walking track passes close to sheer cliff edges and becomes very slippery when wet, so even the walk demands care. The vertical cooling columns visible here are part of what makes the abseiling lines so striking.

### Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck) — closed, and why

Coonowrin, the slender 377 m spire also known as Crookneck, was once a celebrated climbing objective. It is now **closed**. Since March 1999 the Mount Coonowrin section of the park has been a Restricted Access Area under the *Nature Conservation Act 1992*, following a geological stability assessment (the Coffey report, April 1999) that found a high to very high risk of rockfalls from the surrounding cliffs. The closure history is sobering: QPWS records climbing deaths from falls off the north face in the 1970s and early 1980s, a fatal 40 m fall from the start of the Salmons Leap route in 2003, and a 2013 incident in which a climber fell 40 m after loose rock gave way and was critically injured. Entering the restricted area attracts on-the-spot fines (3 penalty units, maximum 80), and rangers patrol the park. The message is simple and non-negotiable: obey the closure and climb elsewhere.

## Respecting Country: cultural protocols at the Glass House Mountains

Long before they were climbed, mapped or named by Lieutenant James Cook in 1770, these peaks were — and remain — sacred Country. They are deeply significant to the Jinibara and Kabi Kabi (Gubbi Gubbi) peoples, who have cared for this land for tens of thousands of years. QPWS notes that the area holds great spiritual significance with many ceremonial sites protected today, and that it was historically a special meeting place where First Nations peoples gathered for ceremony and trade, including the great bunya nut festivals timed to seasonal abundance.

Within that cultural landscape, individual peaks carry particular meaning. In Jinibara and Gubbi Gubbi tradition, Mount Beerwah is understood as the mother of the mountains and Mount Tibrogargan as the father — and these two peaks are among the most culturally sensitive in the park. Traditional Owners have, for many years, asked visitors to consider not climbing the most sacred peaks out of respect for their values. That request sits alongside the legal access framework QPWS administers; both deserve your attention. Native title has also been recognised in this region, affirming the ongoing custodianship of Traditional Owners over Country that includes the Glass House Mountains.

You will also encounter the language of the land on the ground. The Yul-yan-man track, for example, takes its name from the Kabi Kabi language and means "walk slowly" — a fitting instruction for any visitor here. Respecting Country in practice means a few concrete things:

- **Inform yourself before you go.** Read the QPWS "Nature, culture and history" page for the park and take the cultural significance of the peaks seriously in your planning.
- **Weigh the request not to climb the most sacred peaks.** Where Traditional Owners have asked that a peak not be climbed, treat that as a meaningful consideration, not background noise — particularly for Beerwah and Tibrogargan.
- **Leave no trace and protect sites.** Stay on formed tracks and routes, carry out all rubbish, and never disturb ceremonial sites, rock art or cultural features.
- **Tread lightly on the living cliff.** The faces are habitat; avoid disturbing lichens, plants and nesting birds, and never dislodge rock.

Approaching the Glass House Mountains with this awareness is not a box to tick — it is the baseline of being a responsible visitor on someone else's Country.

## The hazards: why this terrain demands respect

QPWS is unusually blunt about the dangers here, and for good reason. The summit routes are explicitly *not* walking tracks; they are unsuitable for young children and inexperienced people who cannot climb unassisted, and serious injuries and deaths have occurred. The major hazards include:

- **Rockfall and loose debris.** Falling rock can occur at any time and in huge amounts; sections of the Tibrogargan and Beerwah routes pass through designated high-risk rockfall zones where you are warned not to linger.
- **Friable, unstable rock.** The volcanic rock can be loose, especially low on the Tibrogargan summit route. Holds can break — one dislodged rock can injure or kill climbers below.
- **Serious exposure and sheer cliff edges.** People who deviate from the route can become trapped on cliffs or in dead-end gullies. Many emergencies here begin with someone straying off-line.
- **Slippery rock when wet.** Smooth volcanic surfaces become dangerously slick in the wet, and rainfall heightens the likelihood of rockfalls and landslides. QPWS is explicit: never climb in wet conditions or if rain is likely.
- **Heat, wind and fading light.** Exposed rock radiates significant heat, raising the risk of heat exhaustion; strong winds and poor visibility in mist or fading daylight add to the danger. Descending in the dark is a recipe for getting cliffed-out.

Because of all this, helmets are essential, you should never climb alone, and you must allow enough time to be off the mountain in daylight — descending can take twice as long as the climb up. Carry a charged phone and a first-aid kit, consider a personal locator beacon on remote routes, and tell a reliable person your plans. Rescues here are genuinely risky for the rescuers, expensive, and can tie up emergency crews for hours.

## Permits, bolts and the rules that actually apply

Recreational climbing and abseiling at the listed locations does not require a special individual permit in the way a commercial operation does, but there are firm rules you must follow — and "permits" is exactly the language people search for, so let's be precise:

- **Restricted Access Areas are legally closed.** Mount Coonowrin is the clearest example. Entering attracts on-the-spot fines under the *Nature Conservation (Protected Areas Management) Regulation 2017*. Closures are not advisory.
- **Bolting is prohibited without written authority.** Installing, replacing or modifying bolts, anchor points or other fixed protection in parks and forests is not permitted without written authority from QPWS. The park contains numerous unapproved anchor points and bolts; QPWS does not recommend relying on them, and using existing fixed gear is at your own risk.
- **Anchor loading limits apply.** QPWS advises that your anchor load must not exceed the weight of one person and their equipment.
- **Commercial and group activities need authorisation.** Guided, commercial or organised group climbing and abseiling on park requires the appropriate QPWS permits — another reason to book through properly authorised, qualified providers rather than going it alone.
- **Leave-no-trace is a rule, not a courtesy.** Minimise vegetation disturbance, stay on formed tracks and routes, be mindful of nesting birds on crags, and carry out all litter.

If you are unsure whether your planned activity needs authorisation, contact QPWS directly before you go.

## Best time to climb: season and weather

The Sunshine Coast hinterland is warm and humid, and that drives the climbing calendar. The cooler, drier months — roughly **April to September** — are by far the best window: more stable weather, less oppressive heat on the exposed rock, and a lower chance of the afternoon storms that build through the warmer half of the year. Summer (roughly December to February) is hot, humid and storm-prone, and the combination of heat radiating off the rock and slippery surfaces after rain makes it the least forgiving time to be on a face.

Whatever the month, the wet is the deal-breaker. Volcanic rock here is treacherous when damp, and rain raises the odds of rockfall and landslide. Always check the Bureau of Meteorology forecast, start early to climb in the cool and finish in daylight, and be willing to walk away if conditions turn. No summit is worth a wet descent on friable rock.

## Getting there, parking and facilities

From Brisbane, it is about 70 km north — close to an hour — via the Bruce Highway, exiting toward the Glass House Mountains and Beerburrum. The trailheads you are most likely to use are:

- **Tibrogargan trailhead** — between Beerburrum and Glass House Mountains townships, accessed via Marshs Road and Barrs Road. This is the start for the Tibrogargan circuit and the Tibrogargan summit route. There are picnic tables and toilets beside the car park, including an accessible toilet cubicle.
- **Beerwah trailhead** — the Beerwah car park is about 9.8 km from Glass House Mountains township via Coonowrin Road and Mount Beerwah Road. Picnic tables and toilets are a short 100 m walk from the car park; the Mount Beerwah view track and summit route start here.
- **Ngungun trailhead** — about 3 km from Glass House Mountains township via Coonowrin Road and Fullertons Road; the starting point for the Ngungun summit walk and roped activity on Ngungun.

Nearby, the Glass House Mountains lookout day-use area (in the adjoining state forest) and the Coochin Creek day-use area offer picnic tables, accessible toilets and gas barbecues, plus some of the best panoramas over the range. Note that the park asks visitors to clean footwear before and after walking to help slow the spread of Phytophthora dieback, a soil-borne plant pathogen — a small step that protects the very plant communities that make these peaks special.

## Climbing safely: get qualified instruction first

Given the seriousness of the terrain, the strongest single piece of advice is this: do not learn to climb or abseil on these peaks unsupervised. The Glass House Mountains reward experience and punish improvisation. If you are building toward outdoor climbing, the sensible progression is to develop core skills in a controlled environment first — indoor walls and supervised sessions — before stepping onto friable, exposed volcanic rock. Our guide to the [best indoor climbing gyms in Australia](/best-indoor-rock-climbing-gyms-in-australia-sydney-melbourne-brisbane-perth/) is a good place to build fitness and rope skills, and Brisbane's own [Kangaroo Point Cliffs](/kangaroo-point-cliffs-climbing-brisbanes-urban-outdoor-gym/) are a far more forgiving, managed urban venue to get comfortable on real rock and at height before heading north.

When you do progress to the Glass House Mountains, go with experienced partners or qualified instruction, carry proper climbing equipment certified to recognised standards, wear a helmet, and follow every QPWS instruction and posted sign. Competence here is not optional — it is the difference between a memorable day and a rescue.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which Glass House Mountains can you climb?

Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service lists Mount Ngungun (253 m), Mount Tibrogargan (364 m) and Mount Beerwah (556 m) as locations for roped climbing and abseiling, strictly for experienced, well-equipped climbers or those under direct supervision. Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck) is closed. Always check current QPWS park alerts before you go, as access can change.

### Is Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck) open for climbing?

No. The Mount Coonowrin section of the park has been a Restricted Access Area under the Nature Conservation Act 1992 since March 1999, due to a high to very high risk of rockfalls identified in a geological report. There is no public access, climbing is not permitted, and on-the-spot fines apply. Choose one of the open locations instead.

### Do I need a permit to climb in the Glass House Mountains?

Recreational climbing and abseiling at the listed peaks does not require an individual permit, but rules apply: bolting needs written QPWS authority, closures such as Mount Coonowrin are legally enforced, and commercial or organised group activities require authorisation. If you are running a guided or group trip, contact QPWS first, or book with a properly authorised provider.

### How should I respect Aboriginal cultural protocols here?

The peaks are sacred to the Jinibara and Kabi Kabi (Gubbi Gubbi) peoples, with Mount Beerwah and Mount Tibrogargan especially significant. Inform yourself via QPWS cultural information, take seriously the request from Traditional Owners to consider not climbing the most sacred peaks, stay on formed routes, never disturb cultural sites, and leave no trace. Respect for Country is the baseline of being a responsible visitor.

### When is the best time to climb the Glass House Mountains?

The cooler, drier months from about April to September are best, offering more stable weather and less heat on the exposed rock. Summer is hot, humid and storm-prone. Never climb in wet conditions — the volcanic rock becomes dangerously slippery and rain heightens rockfall risk. Always check the Bureau of Meteorology forecast and start early to finish in daylight.

### Are the Glass House Mountains dangerous to climb?

Yes — they demand genuine respect. QPWS records that serious injuries and deaths have occurred, with hazards including rockfall, loose and friable rock, sheer exposure, slippery wet surfaces, heat and wind. The summit routes are not walking tracks. Helmets and proper equipment are essential, never climb alone or in the wet, and inexperienced climbers should seek qualified instruction first.
