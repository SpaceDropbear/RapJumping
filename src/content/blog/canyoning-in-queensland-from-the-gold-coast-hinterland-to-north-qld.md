Queensland's canyoning scene doesn't look much like the Blue Mountains or Karijini: instead of sandstone slots or arid red gorges, you're generally in lush rainforest, moving between waterfalls, natural rock slides and clear swimming holes in a warm, tropical or subtropical setting. That makes it a genuinely different, and for many people a more approachable, introduction to canyoning than the colder, more committing canyons further south.

This guide covers Queensland's two best-known canyoning-adjacent destinations (Behana Gorge near Cairns and Cedar Creek Falls in the Gold Coast hinterland), and how they differ from each other.

If waterfall abseiling is the part that appeals most, a guided [rap jumping](/) session is a great way to build abseiling confidence in a fully supervised setting before you tackle a real rainforest gorge.

## Behana Gorge: North Queensland's rainforest canyoning classic

Behana Gorge sits within Wooroonooran National Park, around 35km south of Cairns just past Gordonvale, and is North Queensland's best-known canyoning destination. A roughly 3km rainforest track leads through crystal-clear pools, natural rock chutes and cascading waterfalls to the spectacular Clamshell Falls at the end of the trail. The canyoning experience along the way typically includes abseils beside waterfalls, natural rock slides and swimming, and is generally accessed as a guided tour given the technical sections, remoteness and rainforest terrain involved: a format that suits first-timers, families and anyone keen to try canyoning in a warm, tropical setting rather than the Blue Mountains' cooler, more committing canyons.

## Cedar Creek Falls: a Gold Coast hinterland swimming-hole walk

Cedar Creek Falls, within Tamborine National Park in the Gold Coast hinterland, is a genuinely different kind of destination. It's a 15-20m waterfall reached by a short (roughly 500-900m) walking track, with two or three accessible swimming holes at the base surrounded by subtropical rainforest. Unlike Behana Gorge, Cedar Creek Falls doesn't have formal guided canyoning infrastructure: it's a self-guided swim-and-walk destination rather than a technical abseiling or gorge-descent experience, open to the public from dawn to dusk with no overnight camping. Swimming is allowed but unsupervised, so caution around slippery rocks matters here as much as anywhere else on this list.

## How Queensland canyoning compares

If you're comparing Queensland against the Blue Mountains or Karijini, the biggest practical difference is climate and technicality mix: Queensland's rainforest gorges are warmer year-round and generally shorter, more guided experiences than the Blue Mountains' vast network of independent-access canyons, while Karijini's arid, class-rated gorge system is a different proposition again in terms of remoteness and heat management. ## Season, safety and access

Queensland's wet season, roughly December to March, brings the region's heaviest rainfall and significantly higher water flows through gorges like Behana: more dramatic scenery, but also a materially higher flash-flood risk, and some guided operators scale back or close specific canyons during heavy rain periods as a result. Outside the wet season, conditions are generally more predictable, though tropical downpours can still occur with little warning, so checking current conditions and, for Behana Gorge specifically, going with an experienced guide rather than attempting the technical sections independently is the sensible approach for anyone without prior canyoning experience.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where can I go canyoning in Queensland?

Behana Gorge, in Wooroonooran National Park around 35km south of Cairns, is North Queensland's best-known canyoning destination, with abseils, rock slides and swimming along a rainforest track to Clamshell Falls. Cedar Creek Falls, in the Gold Coast hinterland's Tamborine National Park, is a self-guided swimming-hole and walking destination rather than a technical canyoning site.

### Is Behana Gorge suitable for beginners?

Yes, it's generally considered suitable for first-timers and families when done with an experienced guide, given the guided format and the range of sections involved (abseils, rock slides and swimming).

### Is Cedar Creek Falls a canyoning destination?

Not in the technical sense. Cedar Creek Falls is a self-guided waterfall and swimming-hole walk with no formal canyoning infrastructure or guided abseiling: it suits a casual swim-and-walk visit rather than a technical canyon descent.

### What's the best time of year to go canyoning in Queensland?

Outside the wet season (roughly December to March) generally offers more predictable conditions, since wet-season rainfall significantly raises water flows and flash-flood risk. Tropical downpours can still occur with little warning at any time of year, so always check current conditions before you go.

### How does Queensland canyoning compare to the Blue Mountains?

Queensland's rainforest gorges are generally warmer, shorter and more commonly experienced as guided tours than the Blue Mountains' vast, largely independent-access network of sandstone slot canyons. Both are genuinely different from Karijini's arid, class-rated Pilbara gorge system.

### Do I need a permit to visit Behana Gorge or Cedar Creek Falls?

Day visits to both don't generally require a permit, but always check current requirements with Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service before you go, particularly if you're planning to camp overnight nearby.
