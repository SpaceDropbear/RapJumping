## TL;DR

- The Dave Evans Bicentennial Tree in Warren National Park near Pemberton, Western Australia, is a 75-metre Karri you climb completely unharnessed via 165 metal pegs.
- Pegged in 1988 for Australia's Bicentennial celebrations, it forms a trilogy with the 61-metre Gloucester Tree and 51-metre Diamond Tree, original 1940s fire lookout trees.
- Climbing is free beyond the $12-per-vehicle national park entry, and the trees have recorded no reported fatalities directly from a fall while climbing the pegs.
- The reward is 360-degree views over old-growth Karri forest - and testing your fear of heights first with backward abseiling or Aussie-style rappelling is a wise idea.

## **Where on Earth Can You Climb a 75m Tree Unharnessed?**

The giant Karri tree towers over all who stand before it. At 75 metres tall and more than 250 years old, it must have some stories to tell - particularly when it comes to the people who've dared to climb it.

The spindly metal pegs - all 165 of them - ascend up and around the trunk of the tree like a spiral staircase. With no harness and only a run of flimsy ring-lock wire attached to the outside edge, it's a tourist attraction you'd expect to find deep in the remote forests of South East Asia or South America. At first glance, you'd have to be mad to climb it - but each year, countless **adrenaline** lovers do.

The Dave Evans Bicentennial Tree is located in Warren National Park, just 15 minutes outside the township of Pemberton, in Australia's beautiful southwest Western Australia.

It was first pegged in 1988 as part of Australia's Bicentennial celebrations and was designed to mimic the historic fire lookout trees that were pegged in the Karri Forest during the 1930s and '40s. The Bicentennial Tree forms a trilogy of climbing trees spread across the region, with the 61-metre Gloucester Tree and 51-metre Diamond Tree (both pegged and climbed by thrill-seekers in the same unique way) also popular attractions. These two slightly smaller giants were pegged back in the 1940s and are two of the original eight fire lookout trees.

### **A Different Kind of Vertical Adventure**

As far as **extreme sports** go, Pemberton's climbing trees are certainly something out of the ordinary. The Pemberton climbing trees range between 51 metres and 75 metres high and are public attractions - you complete the climb entirely unharnessed and unsupervised, with prominent warning signs advising of the inherent risks. Apart from standard national park entry fees (which are $12 per vehicle), you don't have to pay to climb these giants.

The Pemberton climbing trees could be considered part of the final frontier; fascinating relics from an era where public liability insurance took a back seat. As you gaze up into the branches, you might wonder what would happen if you reached the top but were too scared to come down. Does the fire brigade even have a ladder that extends halfway up that trunk?

Despite their less-than-safe appearance, historically these trees have maintained an impressive safety record, with no reported fatalities directly from a fall while climbing the pegs. (Though there have been tragic instances of climbers suffering heart attacks upon reaching the ground.) As you place mind over matter and scale the pegs, you begin to realise that it would indeed be hard to fall between the firmly-rammed metal rods. Upon reaching the small tree house in the crown of the canopy, you come to the conclusion that the scariest part is (mostly) in your head; once you're over that inner turmoil that screams ‘should I be doing this?', it's a matter of putting one foot in front of the other and, crucially, not looking down. The climb certainly isn't for the faint-hearted.

### **The View From the Top (and the Bottom!)**

So, is the climb worth it? With 360-degree views of old-growth Karri forest stretching to the horizon, you better believe it. The feeling once your feet reconnect with the earth is unbelievable; If you're an **adventure** seeker heading to Western Australia's south west, Pemberton should absolutely be on your list of stops. Whether you visit one climbing tree or all three, it's well worth it - if not for the views, but to experience something truly unique that you would never expect to come across in the modern era.

Testing and conquering your **fear of heights** before tackling these unharnessed giants is a wise idea. Backward **abseiling** is a great way to cut your teeth, and **Aussie-style rappelling** is sure to get you used to the feeling of **adrenaline** running through your veins! We must admit, even we'd love the chance to tackle Pemberton's giants someday!
