## TL;DR

- Descenders add friction so you can control speed on rope.
- Figure 8: ultra-simple, smooth rappels; no assisted braking; can twist rope.
- ATC: versatile belay/rappel workhorse (single or double ropes); no auto-lock.
- GriGri: assisted braking for extra security; single rope only; heavier/pricier; training required.
- Pick by use case: simplicity (Figure 8), all-round belay+rappel (ATC), added safety/rope access (GriGri).
- Technique still rules: brake-hand discipline, knot rope ends, consider a prusik backup below the device.

If you've done a fair bit of climbing or advanced abseiling, you've probably heard of a descender device. This handy piece of gear helps regulate your speed as you move down a rope, giving you more confidence and control. Whether you're heading out for a canyon adventure or practising complex rescue techniques, choosing the right descender can make all the difference. In this comparison, we'll look at three popular options-the Figure 8, the ATC, and the GriGri-to help you decide which one best fits your style.

## Get to know your descender device

Before we dive into specific models, let's get crystal clear on what a descender device does. In simple terms, it adds friction to your rope so you can control your descent. Some devices, such as Figure 8s and ATCs, rely on a more manual approach. Others, like the GriGri, include assisted braking mechanisms. The more assistance provided, the more gear complexity and weight you typically have to manage.

You'll also note that each device has unique features, from basic friction to auto-locking designs. The main considerations you'll run into are ease of use, safety features, and compatibility with different ropes. If you're relatively new or simply polishing your skill set, a thorough understanding of these devices will help you approach abseiling with greater confidence.

## Compare figure 8, ATC, and GriGri

### Figure 8 basics

If you're after a light, no-frills descender device for classic abseiling or straightforward rappelling, the Figure 8 might be your first pick. Shaped like its name suggests, it's been around for decades-early iterations date back to the 1940s and 1960s. Its popularity has endured because it offers smooth, predictable friction.

- Pros
  - Simple design that's easy to set up
  - Excellent for seamless descents when technique is sound
  - Lightweight and compact
- Cons
  - No assisted braking or self-locking feature
  - Can twist the rope if you're not careful
  - Less forgiving if you lose grip or concentration

Because of its simplicity, the Figure 8 is a favourite among climbers who value minimalism or frequently switch between ropes of varying diameters. It's also a good fit if you already have strong rope-handling habits that keep you in control of your speed.

### ATC in action

ATC stands for Air Traffic Controller, though some just call it a “tubular belay device.” You see these devices everywhere-many climbing gyms keep them on the rental counter because they're incredibly versatile. You can belay a leader, set them up for abseiling, and switch easily between single and double rope configurations.

- Pros
  - Versatile: can handle belaying and rappelling
  - Generally more friction options than a Figure 8
  - Simple and lightweight, making it easy to pack
- Cons
  - No auto-lock or panic brake
  - Technique-dependent for managing friction
  - Lacks the advanced safety features of self-braking descenders

If you love multi-pitch climbing or you move between top-roping and abseiling, an ATC is probably on your harness already. It's a workhorse device that covers a lot of scenarios. Just remember that you provide most of the braking force with your brake hand, so ongoing practice is key. If you're not fully confident in your rope control yet, you might prefer something with a safety catch.

### Why pick a GriGri?

The GriGri is an iconic self-braking descender from Petzl. GriGri devices are often praised for their assisted locking mechanism, which kicks in if the rope moves too quickly. Essentially, it helps “grab” the rope for you, boosting safety during both belaying and descending. The trade-off is that GriGris are a bit heavier and pricier than their simpler counterparts.

- Pros
  - Built-in assisted braking for extra security
  - Great for repeated belays when you need a rest
  - Handy for rope access, rescue scenarios, and leading climbs
- Cons
  - More expensive than an ATC or Figure 8
  - Slightly heavier and bulkier
  - Requires careful training to avoid user error

If you're stepping into more complex climbing or abseiling environments, the GriGri's added security might give you peace of mind. It's also popular for times when you'll be hanging in place often, inspecting gear, or taking photos mid-descent.

## Decide which descender fits you

### Questions to consider

Not sure which option is right for you? Ask yourself these questions:

1. How often do you want an extra safety net?
   - If you like having a device that can lock automatically, the GriGri's assisted braking might be the way to go.
2. Do you need a multipurpose tool?
   - ATCs are great if you frequently switch from belaying a leader to abseiling.
3. Are you keeping weight down for alpine trips?
   - A Figure 8's simpler design might save you a few grams, which can matter on long routes.
4. Will you be doing technical rescues or rope-access work?
   - In that case, assisted-braking devices like the GriGri (or similar self-braking descenders) can offer that extra margin of safety.

### When each device makes sense

- **Figure 8:** Ideal if you're comfortable managing friction with your brake hand, and you want the easiest way to set up a clean, uninterrupted descent.
- **ATC:** A real multi-tasker. If you expect to belay and abseil using the same device, an ATC covers you for most intermediate climbing needs.
- **GriGri:** If you're pushing into advanced territory-longer routes, tricky belays, or rescue work-an assisted brake can be a literal lifesaver. Just make sure you know how to operate it properly.


**What to look for:** specs to match when you buy, rather than specific shop links.

- Black Diamond ATC Guide - a classic tube belay and rappel device with multiple friction modes and a guide mode for bringing up seconds; no assisted braking, so your brake hand does all the work, around $55 to $60.
- Petzl GriGri - assisted braking on single dynamic ropes from 8.5 to 11 mm, suited to belay-heavy days and single-rope descents; single rope only, so it cannot handle double-rope abseils, around $200 to $220.

## Enhance abseiling skills

Owning a great descender device is just one piece of the puzzle. Your abseiling technique, rope management, and anchor setup matter just as much. If you'd like to dig deeper into effective rope handling, anchoring, or advanced abseiling methods, be sure to explore [abseiling techniques](/mastering-abseiling-techniques-and-rope-skills/).

### Rope handling tips

When you rely on friction to keep you safe, it pays to treat your rope with respect. Knotted, dirty, or worn rope will slip more erratically and create unreliable friction. Here are a few pointers:

- **Inspect before every climb:** Look for nicks or flat spots.
- **Keep it clean:** Dirt and grit can wear down your device's friction features and compromise the rope sheath.
- **Practice consistent braking motion:** Avoid jerky movements, which can cause sudden leaps or bounces in your descent.

### Stay safe out there

Even if you own a self-braking device, you can't just rely on the gadget alone. Proper technique is essential, including positioning your brake hand, pacing your descent, and setting up backups like a prusik knot if needed. Also, keep an eye on your environment: loose rock, wet rope, or sharp edges can turn a routine abseil into a white-knuckle experience quickly.

- Always do function checks before abseiling.
- Maintain clear communication with your climbing partner or team.
- If you're new to a particular device, practice at ground level or in a controlled setting before taking it on a big climb.

### FAQs

**Q. Is a GriGri “safer” than an ATC or Figure 8?**  
A. It offers assisted braking, which adds a margin of safety, but misuse still causes accidents. Training and brake-hand discipline are mandatory.

**Q. Can I rappel on a GriGri?**  
A. In specific single-rope setups with proper technique/training, yes. It's not for double-rope rappels and won't fix poor systems.

**Q. Which device is best for beginners?**  
A. An ATC-versatile for gym/crag belays and basic abseils. Learn with a prusik/autoblock backup.

**Q. When should I choose a Figure 8?**  
A. When you want the lightest, simplest rappel device and you're confident managing manual friction.

**Q. What about canyoning?**  
A. ATC or Figure 8 can work, but canyon-specific descenders with multiple friction modes are often better on wet ropes.

**Q. Double-rope rappels-what works?**  
A. ATC and Figure 8 handle doubles. GriGri is single-rope only.

**Q. What backup should I use?**  
A. An autoblock/prusik below the device on the brake strand, clipped to your leg loop.

**Q. Rope diameter compatibility?**  
A. Always check the manufacturer's range for both the device and rope; thin or wet ropes reduce friction.

## Wrapping up

Finding the right descender device depends on how you plan to climb or abseil. A Figure 8 offers classic simplicity for smooth descents, especially if you're comfortable managing friction. An ATC is a do-it-all option that excels in both belaying and abseiling. And for those times when you want extra security-perhaps for trickier routes or rescue work-a GriGri's assisted braking can be a dream.

Remember, device choice alone doesn't guarantee safe or enjoyable descents. You also need a solid rope-handling foundation, quality anchors, and situational awareness. Once you've settled on a device and honed your technique (maybe with a few pointers from your climbing partners), you'll be primed for smooth and controlled abseils every time you head down the rope. Enjoy the adventure, and climb on!
