## TL;DR

- **A "safety-rated" carabiner** is one certified to the European Standard **EN 12275** and the matching **UIAA 121** standard, and stamped with its breaking strength in kilonewtons (kN). A basic connector must hold at least **20 kN** along its major axis with the gate shut.
- **kN means kilonewtons**, a measure of force, not weight. 1 kN is roughly the force of a 100 kg load hanging still, so a 20 kN carabiner withstands about two tonnes before breaking. A real climbing fall rarely exceeds about 5 kN on the climber.
- **Pick the carabiner for the job:** a locking HMS (pear) carabiner for belaying and anchors, lockers for any life-critical connection, and lighter non-locking carabiners on quickdraws.
- **Buy your "personal fit" items** (shoes, harness, helmet) and consider hiring or borrowing the expensive hardware (rope, rack, devices) until you know the climbing you'll actually do.
- **Climbing gear is life-safety equipment.** Inspect it before every use, retire soft goods after a severe fall or chemical/UV damage, and learn to use it under qualified instruction, not from a video.

Climbing gear is the only thing between you and the ground, so it is worth understanding properly rather than buying on price or colour. The good news is that the modern climbing market is heavily standardised: almost every reputable harness, rope, helmet and carabiner sold in Australia is tested against a published European Standard (EN) and, very often, the parallel [UIAA safety standard](https://www.theuiaa.org/safety/safety-standards/). Once you can read those marks, choosing well-made equipment becomes far simpler.

This guide is the gear chapter of our wider [complete guide to rock climbing in Australia](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/). We lead with carabiners and what "safety rated" actually means, because it is the single most misunderstood piece of kit, and then work through the rest of an essential rack, with a buy-or-hire checklist you can take to a shop. It is written for Australian conditions and Australian English, but the standards themselves are international.

One thing up front, and we will repeat it: this is an overview, not a substitute for instruction. The numbers below explain why gear is strong enough; they do not teach you to build an anchor, belay a leader or rig an abseil. For that, get hands-on coaching from a qualified instructor or a structured [beginner pathway](/rock-climbing-for-beginners-in-australia-how-to-get-started/) before you trust your life to any of it.

## Carabiners and safety ratings: the part most people get wrong

A carabiner (also spelt karabiner, and often shortened to "crab" or "biner") is a metal loop with a spring-loaded gate. It is the connector that joins everything in the climbing system, rope to protection, harness to anchor, belay device to belayer. Because so much depends on it, carabiners are the most rigorously specified piece of climbing hardware, and the term "safety rated" has a precise meaning.

### What "safety rated" actually means

For rock climbing, a carabiner is "safety rated" when it is certified to **EN 12275**, the European Standard titled *Mountaineering equipment, Connectors, Safety requirements and test methods*, and usually also to the matching UIAA standard, **UIAA 121**. The two are essentially the same set of tests; the EN standard grew out of the original UIAA work, which dates back to the first karabiner standard the UIAA Safety Commission produced in 1965. A carabiner certified to these standards will be stamped with a **CE** mark, frequently the **UIAA** safety label, and, crucially, its tested strengths in kilonewtons.

This matters because hardware sold for "general use", in hardware shops, camping aisles or as keyring novelties, is usually *not* certified to any climbing standard and may have no meaningful strength rating at all. A keyring carabiner can look almost identical to a climbing one and fail at a fraction of the load. If a carabiner is not marked CE/UIAA and does not carry a kN rating, do not climb on it. Ever.

### What "kN" means on a carabiner

The numbers stamped on the spine are in **kilonewtons (kN)**, a unit of *force*. One kilonewton is about 102 kilograms of static force, so a carabiner rated 20 kN can hold roughly **two tonnes** hanging still before it breaks. That sounds like overkill until you realise climbing falls are dynamic, not static: the energy of a falling body multiplies the force briefly. Even so, the UIAA notes that a real climbing fall, caught on a stretchy dynamic rope, produces a peak force of only around **5 kN** on the climber, and the force on the top piece of protection is very unlikely to exceed about **10 kN**. The generous margin between those numbers and a 20 kN+ carabiner is exactly the point.

For the abseiling side of the same arithmetic, including a kilograms to kilonewtons conversion table, see [the physics of abseiling](/the-physics-of-abseiling-friction-and-force/).

There is an important caveat the UIAA is blunt about. The big safety margin only exists when there is a dynamic rope in the system to absorb energy. If a climber is connected to an anchor by only slings, quickdraws and carabiners, with no rope stretch in the chain, a fall or shock load can generate forces in excess of **25 kN**, which is enough to break a carabiner even with the gate closed. That is why you adjust slings to remove slack at an anchor, and why you never clip into a fixed via-ferrata-style cable with just a sling and carabiner.

### The three numbers: major axis, minor axis, gate open

Every certified carabiner is marked with three strengths, shown as little pictograms next to each figure:

- **Major axis, gate closed** (a vertical double-headed arrow): the strength loaded end-to-end along the spine, the way a carabiner is designed to be loaded. The standard requires a **minimum of 20 kN** for a basic connector; most modern carabiners are rated around **22-25 kN**, and many burly steel or belay carabiners exceed that.
- **Minor axis, gate closed** (a horizontal double-headed arrow): the strength loaded sideways, across the gate and spine, known as cross-loading. This is much weaker, **typically about 7 kN** (the UIAA's worked example for an HMS carabiner shows 10 kN). Cross-loading is a genuine hazard, which is one reason anchors and belays use shapes and lockers that keep the load on the major axis.
- **Open gate** (a carabiner pictogram with the gate ajar): the strength along the major axis when the gate is open, as it can be momentarily during a fall. The standard sets a minimum of **7 kN** for a basic connector (raised from the old 6 kN DIN figure in 1998), and HMS carabiners are often **8-9 kN**. Oval carabiners, used mainly for aid climbing, are allowed as low as 5 kN and are not recommended for catching leader falls.

The UIAA's own advice is practical: if you are heavier than average, carry a lot of gear or climb with a pack, choose carabiners with a higher gate-open strength (8 or 9 kN) for that extra margin. And because the gate-open and cross-loaded figures are so much lower than the major-axis number, the golden rule is to keep carabiners loaded the way they were designed, along the spine, gate shut.

### Carabiner shapes and which to use

Shape changes how a carabiner behaves under load and how easy it is to clip. The four you will meet are:

- **HMS / pear**, a large, symmetrical, pear-shaped carabiner. The wide top suits a *Munter (Italian) hitch* and bulky belay-device set-ups, and it is the standard choice for belaying and master-point anchor connections. In the EN/UIAA scheme this is a **Type H** connector.
- **Offset-D (asymmetric D)**, the most common general carabiner. The D shape pushes the load onto the spine (the strong side) and away from the gate, giving a high strength-to-weight ratio with a usefully large gate opening. Ideal for the rope and bolt ends of quickdraws and for general racking.
- **D**, a symmetrical D. Strong and simple, with a smaller gate opening than an offset-D; you will see these on older quickdraws and some belay set-ups.
- **Oval**, symmetrical and smooth, so loads sit centrally and gear racks neatly; favoured for aid climbing and for holding pulleys or bunches of nuts. As noted, ovals are inherently weaker with the gate open (the EN **Type X**), so they are not a first choice for catching falls.

### Gate types: solid, wiregate, straight and bent

The gate is where most of the design effort goes. The two families are:

- **Solid-gate**, a machined bar gate. Smooth to clip, durable, and often available with a "keylock" nose that has no notch to snag on slings or bolt hangers.
- **Wiregate**, the gate is a loop of stainless wire. It is lighter, and because the moving mass is so small it is far less prone to "gate flutter" (the gate whipping open from inertia during a fall). The UIAA specifically notes wiregates significantly reduce the chance of an inadvertent gate-open failure, though on very crystalline or knobbly rock a wire can sometimes be nudged open against an edge.

Gates also come **straight** or **bent**. A bent gate scoops the rope in and makes clipping faster, so it lives on the *rope end* of a quickdraw; the straight gate goes on the *bolt end*, clipped to the hanger. Mixing them up (clipping the rope into the bolt-end carabiner that has been scraping on steel) is how grooves and sharp edges end up cutting rope sheaths, the UIAA recommends keeping dedicated rope-end and gear-end carabiners.

### Locking vs non-locking

A **non-locking** carabiner is fast to clip and lives where speed matters and a failure is not catastrophic, chiefly on quickdraws between bolts, where the rope keeps the system dynamic. A **locking** carabiner has a sleeve that secures the gate shut, and is used wherever a gate coming open would be serious: belaying, abseiling, anchors, lower-offs and any single critical connection. Locking mechanisms include:

- **Screwgate**, a threaded sleeve you screw closed by hand. Simple, reliable and easy to inspect; the trade-off is you must remember to do it up, and check it has not vibrated loose.
- **Twist-lock / auto-lock**, the sleeve springs and twists shut on its own when you release the gate. Faster and harder to forget, at the cost of more moving parts; two- and three-stage versions resist accidental opening better.

The UIAA recommends a locker (or two non-lockers with gates opposed and reversed) wherever high reliance is placed on a single carabiner, a belay, a lower-off, a vital runner, or any carabiner on a long sling that might whip against rock. When abseiling or belaying with a figure-of-eight device, stay vigilant: the device can lever a screwgate open in an "abnormal" loading, so keep it clear of the gate.

### So what is the best carabiner for rock climbing?

There is no single best carabiner, only the best one for the job, but if you are buying your first locker, a **locking HMS (pear) carabiner certified to EN 12275 / UIAA 121, with a screwgate or twist-lock and a keylock nose**, is the most versatile choice. It belays, takes a Munter hitch as a back-up, and connects you to an anchor. Add a couple more lockers for anchor building, and reserve lightweight non-locking offset-D or wiregate carabiners for your quickdraws. Whatever you buy, confirm the CE/UIAA marks and the kN figures are present before it leaves the shop.

## The essential rock-climbing rack: a buy-or-hire checklist

Beyond carabiners, a starter kit is short. Here is the core gear, the standard each item is tested to, whether to buy it or hire it first, and a realistic working lifespan. Treat the lifespans as guidance, manufacturers' instructions and the condition of the item always take priority, and any severe fall or damage retires soft goods immediately.

| Item | Certification standard | Buy or hire first? | Typical lifespan (with care) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carabiners (locking & non-locking) | EN 12275 / UIAA 121 | Buy, start with 2-3 lockers | Indefinite if undamaged (20+ years); retire on cracks, deep wear, sticky gates |
| Harness | EN 12277 (Type C sit harness) | Buy, must fit you | Up to ~10 years unused; ~3-5 years in regular use; sooner if worn |
| Dynamic rope | EN 892 (single / half / twin) | Hire or share first | Up to ~10 years stored; 1-3 years with frequent use; retire after a severe fall |
| Belay device | Sold as PPE; pairs with EN 12275 carabiner | Buy, it is cheap and personal | Years; retire on grooving, sharp edges or excessive wear |
| Helmet | EN 12492 / UIAA 106 | Buy, must fit you | ~5-10 years; retire after any significant impact |
| Climbing shoes | No safety standard (performance item) | Buy, fit is everything | Until worn through; resoleable |
| Slings & quickdraws | EN 566 (slings) + EN 12275 (carabiners) | Hire/share first, then buy | Soft goods up to ~10 years; retire after a severe fall or UV/chemical damage |
| Nuts (chocks) | EN 12270 | Hire/borrow a rack first | Indefinite if undamaged; check wires and swages |
| Cams (frictional anchors) | EN 12276 | Hire/borrow first, expensive | Many years; retire on frayed slings, bent stems or worn lobes |
| Personal anchor / lanyard | EN 566 (sling-based) where applicable | Buy once climbing multi-pitch | Soft goods up to ~10 years; retire after shock loading |
| Chalk & chalk bag | n/a (not life-safety) | Buy | Indefinite |

### Harness (EN 12277)

Your harness ties you into the rope, so it is a buy-and-fit item, not something to borrow blind. The relevant standard, **EN 12277**, defines several types: **Type A** full-body, **Type B** small/child harnesses (for lighter users), **Type C** the adult sit harness most climbers use, and **Type D** a chest harness used with a sit harness. A certified Type C sit harness is tested to withstand at least **15 kN** at the tie-in points, far beyond any force a fall could realistically apply. That is why harnesses have never been known to fail from lack of strength. Choose one that fits snugly above the hips, sits comfortably when you hang in it, and has the right number of gear loops for your climbing. Try before you buy.

### Dynamic rope (EN 892)

A climbing rope is "dynamic", it stretches to absorb the energy of a fall, which is precisely what keeps peak forces down around that 5 kN figure. (This is the opposite of a static or "low-stretch" rope, which is for abseiling, hauling and rigging, not for catching lead falls.) The standard is **EN 892**, and it defines three categories, each marked on the rope's end label:

- **Single rope** (a circled "1"), used on its own. The standard caps the peak impact force at **12 kN** in the first standard drop test with an 80 kg mass, and requires the rope to hold at least five such drops. Most sport and gym climbing uses a single rope, commonly **9.4-10.2 mm** diameter and **60-70 m** long for crags (a 70 m rope is a sensible default for many Australian sport areas; always check the route length).
- **Half rope** (a circled "½"), used as a pair, clipping the two strands alternately into protection. Tested with a 55 kg mass, with a lower impact-force limit of **8 kN**. Favoured for wandering trad lines and reducing rope drag.
- **Twin rope** (a circled infinity symbol), also a pair, but both strands clip into every piece. Tested together with an 80 kg mass and must survive more drops. A lightweight option for alpine and long routes.

For a first rope, a single rope around 9.8-10 mm balances durability and handling, and a length matched to your local crags. Hiring or sharing a rope at first is sensible: ropes are expensive, wear with use, and the right diameter and length depend entirely on the climbing you settle into.

### Belay device

A belay device creates friction so you can hold a falling climber and lower them under control. Two broad families:

- **Tubular devices**, a simple slotted tube the rope bends through. Light, versatile, work with a wide range of rope diameters, and many have a "guide mode" for bringing up a second on multi-pitch. They rely entirely on the belayer keeping a hand on the brake strand.
- **Assisted-braking devices**, these add a camming or pinching action that helps lock the rope under a sudden load. They add a margin of safety, especially for single-pitch sport belaying, but they are not "automatic" and still demand correct technique and a hand on the brake at all times.

Either way, the device pairs with a locking **HMS carabiner**, and it is cheap enough to own from the start. Learn the specific device under instruction, they are not all operated the same way.

### Helmet (EN 12492)

A climbing helmet protects against two things: falling rock or dropped gear from above, and your head striking the wall in a fall or a swing. The standard is **EN 12492** (with the parallel UIAA 106), which tests shock absorption, penetration resistance and the chinstrap. Under impact testing the force transmitted to the head must stay below **10 kN**. Climbing helmets are tested for impacts from several directions, not just the crown, which is why a general-purpose industrial hard hat is not an equivalent. Fit matters: it should sit level, not tip back, and stay put with the strap done up. Retire a helmet after any significant impact, even if it looks fine.

### Climbing shoes

Shoes are the one core item with no safety standard, they are pure performance, gripping the rock with a sticky rubber sole. Fit is everything: a snug shoe (not painfully tight) transmits power to small holds. Beginners do well in a flatter, more comfortable shoe; aggressive downturned shoes are for steep, hard climbing. They are personal and should be bought, not borrowed long term, though gyms and guided operators supply them for first sessions.

### Chalk and chalk bag

Magnesium carbonate chalk keeps your hands dry for grip, carried in a waist-worn chalk bag (or a larger bucket for bouldering). Not life-safety gear, simply useful. Note that some Australian crags and gyms restrict or ban loose chalk for environmental or visual-impact reasons, so check local etiquette and consider liquid chalk where required.

### Slings and quickdraws (EN 566 / EN 12275)

A **sling** (or runner) is a sewn loop of nylon or Dyneema/Spectra tape used to extend protection, build anchors and tie off features. The standard, **EN 566**, requires a minimum breaking strength of **22 kN**. A **quickdraw** is two carabiners joined by a short sewn sling, so it is governed by both EN 566 (the dogbone) and EN 12275 (the carabiners). Remember the earlier warning: slings barely stretch, so a fall directly onto a sling with no rope in the system can generate very high forces. Keep slings as part of a dynamic chain, and replace them well within their lifespan.

### Trad protection: nuts (EN 12270) and cams (EN 12276)

Traditional ("trad") climbing means placing your own removable protection in cracks and removing it afterwards, rather than clipping fixed bolts. The two staples:

- **Nuts (chocks)**, wedges of alloy on a wire, slotted into constrictions. Certified to **EN 12270**, with breaking strengths that scale with size (smaller nuts are weaker, larger ones stronger).
- **Cams (frictional anchors)**, spring-loaded camming devices that expand into parallel-sided cracks. Certified to **EN 12276**, which tests them at both a marginal (25% retracted) and a solid (75% retracted) placement; the minimum rating under the standard is **5 kN**, though most modern cams in usable sizes are rated well above that.

Australia has some of the world's great trad, the orange cliffs of [Mt Arapiles (Dyurrite)](/climbing-at-mt-arapiles-australias-trad-climbing-mecca/) chief among them, but trad is a judgement-heavy discipline. Placing gear that will actually hold a fall is a skill learned over time with a mentor or instructor, not from a kit list. Borrow or hire a rack before committing several hundred dollars to your own.

### Personal anchor

Once you move onto multi-pitch routes, you need a reliable way to clip yourself to the anchor while you belay and swap over. Options range from simply tying in with the climbing rope (a clove hitch to the anchor), to a dedicated sling-based personal anchor system. Sling-based anchors are soft goods governed by EN 566 and have the same low-stretch caveat, never let one take a shock load with slack in it. As with everything here, learn the technique before you rely on it.

## Inspection, care and retirement: when to trust it, when to bin it

Buying certified gear is only half the job; keeping it serviceable is the other half. The UIAA's guidance, drawn from its member federations, is consistent across metal and textile gear: inspect before every use, and retire on damage or doubt.

### Metal gear (carabiners, belay devices, cams)

Well-treated carabiners can last decades, the UIAA notes there is no reason a cared-for carabiner should not have a working life of at least 20 years. Check before use that the gate opens and closes smoothly and snaps fully shut on its own; that screwgate and twist-lock sleeves operate cleanly and lock reliably; and that the inside radius where the rope runs is free of burrs, grooves or sharp edges (which can shred a rope sheath). Retire a carabiner with a sticky or non-closing gate, visible cracking, deep wear (more than about 1 mm), or corrosion you cannot rule out as harmless, sea-cliff salt corrosion is a known killer. Never file out a sharp edge or bend a gate back into shape; that is not a repair.

### Soft goods (rope, harness, slings, quickdraw slings)

Textiles have a finite life whether you use them or not. Even unused and well stored, ropes, harnesses and slings are generally considered to have a maximum life of about **10 years** from manufacture; in regular use that drops sharply, a frequently used rope might last one to three years. Retire soft goods immediately after a **severe fall** (a high fall-factor load), or if they show cuts, a furry or glazed sheath, core you can feel through the sheath, stiff or fused sections, or any contact with chemicals, particularly acids and solvents, which can destroy nylon invisibly. UV degrades textiles too, so store gear cool, dark and dry, away from car boots and garages where battery acid and fuels lurk.

### The cardinal rule

If you are ever unsure whether a piece of life-safety gear is sound, retire it. Hardware is replaceable; you are not. And no inspection routine substitutes for knowing how to use the gear correctly in the first place, which brings us back to instruction.

## Buy or hire? A sensible order for spending

You do not need a full rack to start, and buying everything at once is a fast way to spend a lot on gear that may not suit the climbing you grow into. A practical order:

- **Buy first:** climbing shoes, a harness and a helmet, the personal-fit items you will use in every session and at every gym.
- **Buy soon:** a belay device and a couple of locking HMS carabiners, cheap, personal, and needed the moment you belay.
- **Hire, borrow or share at first:** the rope, quickdraws and any trad rack. These are expensive, wear with use, and the right choice depends on whether you settle into sport, trad or multi-pitch. Gyms and guided operators supply all of it while you learn.

Buy certified gear from a reputable climbing retailer, and avoid second-hand soft goods (rope, harness, slings) unless you know the item's full history, you cannot see fatigue or chemical damage, and you are trusting your life to a stranger's storage habits. Second-hand metal can be acceptable if you can inspect it thoroughly, but when in doubt, buy new.
