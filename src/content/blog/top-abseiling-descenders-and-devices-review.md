## TL;DR

A quality rappelling device balances friction control, durability, and rope compatibility.

- **Figure-8s:** Simple, versatile, great for canyoning and smooth rope flow, but no assisted braking.
- **Tube devices:** Lightweight, affordable, versatile for single/double ropes, require constant brake-hand control.
- **Guide mode tubes:** Ideal for multi-pitch climbs, more options but more complex to use.
- **Assisted braking devices:** Built-in camming or brake assist, safer for slips, heavier and pricier.
- **Safety:** Always look for UIAA or CE certification and maintain backups like an autoblock hitch.

## Explore top abseiling gear

There is a surprising variety of abseiling gear on the market, each with its own design quirks. Some devices excel in multi-pitch scenarios, others are perfect for beginners who need maximum braking assistance, and still others work wonderfully for canyoning, where you might face wet, muddy conditions.

Here is what you should look out for when you research abseiling gear:

- Strong braking capabilities to reduce the risk of runaway descents.
- Smooth rope feed that will not snag or twist too easily.
- Durability and resistance to wear, including ease of cleaning.
- User-friendly operation that suits your experience level.
- Certifications, such as those from the UIAA (International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation) or CE (European Commission), which verify that the equipment has undergone rigorous testing.

Getting to know the different types of devices will be your first step, so you can narrow down your choices to the style that suits you best.

## Understand what makes a great device

Rappelling can seem simple, but each component in your setup serves an important purpose. Your descender is crucial because it regulates friction and brake force, and you do not want to rely on a single bolt or anchor that is not secure enough.

Below are some key elements of an effective abseiling device:

1. Friction control:  
   Managing friction is what keeps your descent steady. Devices come in different shapes and configurations to offer varying degrees of friction. Tube devices, figure-8s, and assisted braking designs each handle friction in a distinct way.
2. Build and weight:  
   Lightweight gear is more comfortable to carry on multi-pitch climbs or long approaches. For heavy-duty or repeated use, some climbers do not mind extra weight if it delivers added brake control.
3. Backup and belay integration:  
   Many rappelling devices double as belay devices for climbing. Assisted braking belay devices tend to be heavier but offer a catch mechanism when the climber falls. Tube or figure-8 devices are simpler and lighter, but they rely heavily on your belay technique.
4. Certification standard:  
   Checking for proper certification can save you from trusting sub-par gear. The UIAA and CE labels indicate that the device meets minimum strength and safety requirements. Before you drop in on a new canyon or abseil down a cliff, confirm that your gear meets these reliability standards.
5. Compatibility with ropes and conditions:  
   Some devices perform better on wet ropes or with certain rope diameters. For canyoning, specialised figure-8s offer multiple friction settings, and many canyoneers bring gloves to protect against rope burn. You also want to look at the manufacturer's guidelines to ensure it is suitable for rappelling, not just belaying.

## Compare popular descenders

Below, you will find a closer look at the most common types of descenders and belay devices used for abseiling. Each category has its strengths and weaknesses, so have a think about which features are non-negotiable for you.

### Figure-8 devices

Figure-8 devices are a classic choice. Shaped like, well, a figure-eight, these devices offer a simple method of threading the rope so it generates friction. They are particularly popular for:

- Canyoning descents:  
  Many figure-8s allow you to twist and lock the rope, creating more or less friction as needed.
- Smooth rope flow:  
  The wide metal ring helps reduce rope kinks.

However, figure-8 devices can twist ropes if you are not mindful, and they do not include an assisted brake mechanism. If your top priority is straightforward, reliable friction, a figure-8 is worth a look.

### Tube belay devices

Tube belay devices, such as the iconic Black Diamond ATC, gained popularity in the 90s. Many climbers still trust them for belaying on rock climbs. They also work for rappelling, especially in multi-pitch settings. Key advantages include:

- Versatility:  
  Tube devices can handle single, double, or half ropes effectively, depending on the model.
- Lightweight build:  
  Typical designs weigh as little as 60 grams, making them easy to carry in any pack.

On the downside, they require constant brake-hand diligence because there is no mechanical assist.

### Guide mode tube belay devices

Guide mode tube devices are an evolution of the typical tube design. They feature an extra attachment point that allows you to belay directly off an anchor when you bring up your second. For rappelling:

- Multi-pitch convenience:  
  You can belay two followers at the same time, then use the same device to rappel in a pinch.
- Extra slots and holes:  
  These can reduce friction or offer additional rope-managing options.

While these devices are fantastic for multi-pitch climbs, they can be overkill if you only plan to abseil casually at a single crag. They require practice and a full understanding of their geometry to avoid accidental rope jams.

### Assisted braking devices

Assisted braking devices are the new standard at many climbing gyms, and they are quickly becoming a go-to for outdoor routes too. Devices like the Petzl GriGri, Trango Vergo, or Mad Rock Lifeguard offer:

- Strong braking forces:  
  If your brake hand slips or you get surprised by a sudden jerk, the camming mechanism helps hold the rope.
- Reduced grip fatigue:  
  They allow you to lock off the rope without bearing all the load in your hand.

However, they are often heavier and more expensive. For rappelling, you can still use them by threading the rope as per the manufacturer's instructions, but keep in mind that assisted braking devices can make it tougher to provide a fluid, soft catch if you are belaying on lead. On the bright side, if you prefer to do a lot of single-pitch cragging, an assisted brake can be a life-changer.

## Check essential certifications

As you narrow down your contenders, double-check that your top picks meet important industry standards:

- UIAA (International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation):  
  Though not mandatory to sell in most places, UIAA certification is highly regarded. The UIAA sets global standards for climbing gear, and you can browse their Certified Equipment database to confirm which brands and models hold the UIAA Safety Label.
- CE (European Commission) certification:  
  This is required for climbing gear sold in Europe. CE-rated devices abide by minimal safety requirements, including tests for tensile strength and durability.
- Additional testing programs like ISO:  
  Some brands also partner with independent groups (like ISO) that audit and confirm that the product meets ongoing quality checks. This can provide extra reassurance that you are purchasing reliable gear.

One rule stands tall: steer clear of suspiciously cheap, non-certified gear for personal protective equipment (PPE). It may not have undergone the rigorous testing that ensures your safety on the wall.

## Stay safe while descending

Once you have selected your ideal device, you still need good habits to keep your descents incident-free. The American Alpine Club found that three primary causes were responsible for about 75% of all rappelling accidents. These include not using a backup knot, failing to extend the rappel system, and improper anchor setups.

Here are a few pointers that will give you confidence every time you head over the edge:

- Use a backup knot or friction hitch:  
  An Autoblock hitch or Prusik loop is an excellent choice. With a 24- to 36-inch cord, you can tie a friction hitch on the brake strand below your descender. If your brake hand slips, the knot will catch the rope.
- Extend your device:  
  Positioning your rappel device away from your harness, usually with a sling or a personal anchor system (PAS), helps prevent the brake hitch from jamming against the device.
- Inspect anchor points:  
  Never rely on a single bolt or point of fixation. Check every anchor for wear, corrosion, or signs of weakness. Natural anchors, like sturdy trees or boulders, should also be tested by giving them a firm tug or two.
- Test before detaching:  
  Clip into your personal anchor, set up the rappel, and give the ropes a careful tension test. Do not remove your tether until you are sure the ropes are threaded and everything is secure.
- Pay attention to rope ends:  
  Tie stopper knots at the ends of your rope, especially if you are not certain about the descent length. Loose rope ends that slip through your descender can result in a big fall.
- Check your rope condition:  
  Sun exposure and moisture can degrade ropes over time. Replace suspect ropes rather than risking a fall due to wear-related failure.
- Keep an eye on friction and heat:  
  Long descents can generate heat in your device, especially figure-8s. Consider bringing rappel gloves if you are doing multiple descents or dealing with abrasive, wet canyon terrain.

### FAQs

**Q: What is the safest rappelling device for beginners?**  
A: Assisted braking devices like the Petzl GriGri offer added safety by locking when your brake hand slips.

**Q: Are figure-8 devices still worth using?**  
A: Yes-especially for canyoning and long descents where smooth rope flow is important, but they require strong brake-hand skills.

**Q: What's the lightest rappelling device option?**  
A: Tube devices like the Black Diamond ATC can weigh as little as 60 g, making them great for long approaches.

**Q: Can I rappel with a belay device?**  
A: Yes-many belay devices (tube, guide mode, assisted braking) double as rappel devices when used properly.

**Q: What rope diameter should I match with my device?**  
A: Check the manufacturer's guidelines, most handle 8-11 mm, but canyoning or rescue devices may have wider ranges.

If you value simplicity and do not mind keeping your brake hand firmly in control, a tube belay device might be your best friend. If you are heading for a canyoning adventure, a figure-8 with optional friction settings could be just the ticket. And if you want modern convenience and love the feeling of an automatic backup, an assisted braking device is tough to beat.

Also, do not forget that your entire system matters. Picking the best device is just part of the puzzle. You need well-maintained ropes, a solid harness, reliable anchor points, and thoughtful technique. If you want a broader look at harnesses, slings, and more, check out our abseiling gear resource. Spend time practising with a partner so you can gain confidence in using your descender, before you are perched atop a big drop.
