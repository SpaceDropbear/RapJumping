When you trust abseil anchors with your life, it pays to know exactly how to build and check them. Whether you're descending a simple cliff or making your way down a multi-pitch route, an anchor is your lifeline. In this tutorial, you'll learn how to select solid anchors, secure your ropes, and introduce safety backups, so you can abseil with greater confidence.

## Gather your essential gear

Before you start, make sure you have all the items you need. Having the right gear from the outset helps prevent shortcuts and oversights.

1. 6 mm Accessory Cord
   - Handy for creating fresh anchor loops or for replacing old in-situ cord.
   - Keep at least a few metres available.
2. Locking Carabiners
   - Two or more screwgate carabiners are typically enough for standard descents.
   - They prevent rope slippage and unintended gate opening.
3. Belay/Abseil Device
   - A device with smooth friction control is essential for safe, controlled descents.
   - Double-check the device is compatible with the diameter of your ropes.
4. Prusik Cord
   - Used to create an auto-block (a backup that locks if you let go).
   - Typically 5 or 6 mm in diameter and long enough for multiple wraps around both ropes.
5. Proper Rope(s)
   - Inspect for frays or damage before every outing.
   - If you're using two ropes, try matching diameters to ensure they pull smoothly.

By organising these essentials in advance, you're already setting yourself up for a safer abseil experience.

## Find and inspect anchor points

Your anchor's quality is the heart of any safe descent. Different scenarios call for different setups, but the main principle is always the same: choose solid, reliable anchor points.

### Natural single-point anchors

- Trees: Use only healthy trunks at least 20 cm in diameter (about the thickness of your thigh). Test for movement by giving the trunk a good push.
- Rocks or boulders: Make sure they're large, stable, and not fractured. Walk around to confirm they won't shift under load.
- Rock threads: Sometimes you'll find a natural hole or tunnel in the rock. Thread your 6 mm cord right through it for a neat wrap that stays put.

### Fixed or bolted anchors

- Bolts: Look for bolts of 3/8 inches (10 mm) or thicker, set firmly in solid rock.
- Hardware: Check any Maillons, rings, or chains for rust, deep grooves, or cracks. If you see serious wear, consider adding a backup carabiner.
- Pitons (less common these days): Tap with a carabiner or small rock to see if it rings solidly. A dull sound might indicate a loose or corroded piton.

### Gear-based anchors

- Wires and cams: If you're carrying trad gear, place a few pieces in solid cracks.
- Equalization: Tie them together with 6 mm cord so they share the load. An overhand knot works well to form a master point.

Make a habit of checking that the anchor isn't compromised by corrosion, weather damage, or questionable rock quality. If anything looks shaky, move on or build a multi-point anchor for added security.

## Create single point anchors

Single point anchors can be super quick and effective, especially when you have a bomber tree or solid boulder. If the anchor spot is popular, you might notice existing cord or a steel carabiner.

1. Secure your accessory cord
   - Wrap the cord around the anchor (tree trunk, boulder, or rock horn).
   - Tie it firmly with a double fisherman's knot (or a sturdy overhand if the cord ends are long enough).
2. Inspect any in-situ cord
   - Look for fraying, discolouration, or stiff spots.
   - If the material is questionable, replace it with your own.
   - Watch for “fabric on fabric” friction. If the rope rubs directly on the sling, consider adding a carabiner or ring.
3. Thread or clip your rope
   - If there's a steel Maillon or old screwgate in place, pass your rope through it to reduce direct cord wear.
   - If not, you may clip two opposite-and-opposed carabiners into the cord loop, then feed the rope through carabiners.

Single point anchors can be surprisingly strong if you pick the right anchor. However, if you have any doubts, use a multi-point design.

## Set up multi-point anchors

Sometimes, one good anchor isn't available, or you just want extra redundancy. This is where you combine gear or use multiple natural features to build a more robust setup.

1. Choose two or three solid placements
   - For trad gear, mix and match the sizes of cams, wires, or hexes so you don't use up all of your gear for one anchor. You might need those finger-sized pieces later.
2. Connect with your cord
   - Arrange your pieces in a way that roughly equalizes the load. A loop of 6 mm cord or a cordelette works great.
   - Tie an overhand knot to create a master point. Keep the angles between pieces under about 60 degrees to reduce tension on each anchor.
3. Avoid total gear overkill
   - Use only what you need to feel safe. Leaving behind minimal gear is wise for a long descent so you can build more anchors below.
   - Make sure everything is snug but not so tight that shifting the rope or anchor is impossible.

This approach is often used on multi-pitch descents, where you might not have large trees or big boulders at each station.

## Avoid friction issues

Nylon-on-nylon contact can generate heat quickly and damage your cord. A small detail like friction can undermine the best planning if overlooked:

- Place a carabiner where the rope would rub on your sling or cord.
- Double-check any webbing running over sharp edges. Pad them with a spare clothing item or gear if needed.
- If you see metal links (like Maillons) on existing anchors, thread your ropes through them instead of the cord.

This reduces the risk of cord meltdown or rope damage when you pull your lines after the abseil.

## Attach ropes safely

Now it's time to set up the rope. Sloppy rope management can lead to entanglements, uneven tails, or downright dangerous slip-ups.

1. Thread the anchor point
   - Pass one end of your rope through the anchor ring, carabiners, or cord loop.
   - If using two ropes, align their midpoints at the anchor if you're doing a double-rope abseil.
2. Tie stopper knots
   - Stopper knots at the rope ends are a must. They'll jam in your belay device if you accidentally abseil past the rope's end.
   - This step is crucial. In 2007, a climber named Lara Kellog died after rappelling off the end of her rope. Such tragedies highlight why stopper knots matter.
3. Match rope lengths
   - Confirm both rope ends touch the ground or the next station.
   - If there's a big height difference, readjust until the lengths match, so you don't end up with one too-short strand.

If you're doing a single-rope abseil, you'll just feed the rope until the midpoint is at the anchor. For double-rope setups, tie the rope ends together with a neat knot (usually an overhand knot) and test it by giving each side a strong pull.

## Add a backup system

A safety backup protects you and makes others more comfortable, especially if you're teaching or abseiling with less experienced friends.

- Prusik knot: Wrap a short prusik cord around both ropes below your belay device, clip the free end to your leg-loop using a locking carabiner, and you have an auto-block that catches if you let go.
- Fireman's belay: If you're particularly nervous, have a partner rappel first. They stay at the bottom, lightly holding both rope strands. If you lose control, they can pull down on the ropes to slow or stop your descent.

Using a backup doesn't replace good technique, but it can make a huge difference when you're tired, scared, or messing with an unfamiliar descent.

## Double-check everything

Momentary complacency causes a surprising number of accidents, so make a final inspection before you unclip.

- Anchor solidity: Is every piece (or tree/boulder) still locked in place with no movement?
- Rope through anchor: Confirm the rope is correctly threaded or clipped into a carabiner.
- Belay device: Ensure it's set up on the rope the right way, with locks tightened.
- Backup knot: Is your prusik properly wrapped, or is someone giving a fireman's belay?
- End knots: Are there stopper knots at each rope end?
- Harness attachment: Re-check your harness buckle and the screwgate on your belay loop.

At this stage, it's wise to keep a firm grip on your ropes, especially if you're near a cliff edge. Only detach from the anchor once everything else is locked and loaded.

## Descend with confidence

Abseiling can feel thrilling but requires a calm approach. Here's a quick rundown:

1. Lean back into your harness
   - Let the weight settle comfortably, so you're not fighting gravity.
   - Keep your feet planted on the rock or slope, with a slight bend in your knees.
2. Control friction
   - Allow the rope to slide gradually through your abseil device.
   - Keep your brake hand below the device. If you need to stop, move your brake hand to your hip or tie off the device.
3. Go at your own pace
   - You don't need to rush. If you're uncertain, pause, check your position, and communicate with your climbing partner.
   - If you feel your friction is too low or you're zipping down too fast, add an extra carabiner or use your backup prusik.
4. Stay alert
   - Watch for loose rock or unstable ledges.
   - Keep your rope free of tangles as much as possible so you don't wedge it into cracks when you pull it later.

## Retrieve ropes smoothly

Once you're safely on the ground or at the next station, you'll need to pull the ropes. Tangled ropes and excessive friction are common frustrations.

- Look for direct lines: If the rope runs around a corner or over a bulge, it might jam when you pull. Adjust your angle if possible.
- Tug firmly but steadily: Short, sharp yanks are more likely to wedge the rope.
- Manage leftover rope: Coil or flake the rope to prevent knots, especially if you still have more pitches to go.

If the rope snags, try to give it some slack, reposition yourself, and pull from a slightly different angle. You do not want to climb back up to free a jammed rope unless you absolutely have to.

### FAQ's

**Q. What makes a good abseil anchor?**  
A. Solid, stable, and redundant - healthy trees, secure bolts, or well-placed trad gear, all in good condition.

**Q. How do I prevent rope damage on an anchor?**  
A. Avoid nylon-on-nylon contact by threading through metal links or adding a carabiner at friction points.

**Q. Should I use single or multi-point anchors?**  
A. Single points work if they're truly bomber; otherwise, build a multi-point anchor for redundancy.

**Q. Why tie stopper knots in my rope?**  
A. They prevent rappelling off the end - a leading cause of serious abseil accidents.

**Q. What backups can I use during descent?**  
A. Common options are an auto-block prusik under your device or a fireman's belay from a partner below.
