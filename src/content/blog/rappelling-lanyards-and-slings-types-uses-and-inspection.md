## Meet rappelling lanyards

A lanyard is a flexible piece of rope, webbing, or cable that keeps you attached to your anchor or lifeline. Unlike simple tethers, these lanyards often feature connectors, such as carabiners or snaphooks, at each end. They're designed so you can safely adjust your attachment point, work your way around ledges, and maintain security during descents.

Rappelling lanyards are typically made from dynamic or semi-dynamic materials that can absorb some of the shock in a fall. In climbing parlance, dynamic materials stretch under load to reduce the impact on your body and your anchor. A static material, on the other hand, doesn't stretch and can transmit higher forces if you slip. For abseiling enthusiasts, a properly rated lanyard can mean the difference between a smooth rappel and a jolting surprise.

### Why WLL and MBS matter

You might notice two common terms when shopping for any climbing or abseiling gear:

- Working Load Limit (WLL): This rating appears more often on rapid links (also called rapides or quick links). It indicates the maximum weight the product can lift under specific conditions.
- Minimum Breaking Strength (MBS): This is usually found on climbing or personal protection equipment, reflecting the load at which the device is likely to fail.

Rappelling gear is often labeled with MBS because you need a clear measure of how much force it can handle before breaking. Rapid links, on the other hand, often show a WLL plus a safety factor (often 5x the WLL). In a fall, you generate a force called the Fall Impact Load, which can exceed that limit if your equipment is at its limit-or if it's poorly maintained.

## Explore different lanyard types

Not all lanyards are built the same. Different designs suit different scenarios, so it helps to know which arrangement supports your typical style of abseiling or climbing.

### Shock absorbing lanyards

A shock absorbing lanyard is often used in fall-arrest systems. It includes a built-in shock absorber (some use a pack or bungee-style design) that engages when you fall, reducing the peak force on your body and anchor. Although these are more common in industrial or construction settings, you may encounter them in rope access or big-wall climbing where a surprise slip is more likely.

- Pros: Great for huge falls, extra protection for anchors and your spine.
- Cons: Bulkier design, not as common purely for abseiling.

### Positioning lanyards

You might see positioning lanyards labeled for tree work or construction, but they can be useful for abseiling too. They're designed to hold you in a certain spot, such as when you want both hands free to set up your rappel device. Generally, they aren't meant to catch a big fall, so be aware of their limits.

- Pros: Keeps you stable while you organise rope or gear.
- Cons: Limited shock absorbers, so not ideal for significant fall distances.

### Adjustable lanyards

Adjustable lanyards give you control over length. Devices like the Petzl Connect Adjust or Camp Swing let you quickly tweak your distance from the anchor. These rope-based models tend to be dynamic, significantly reducing force if you slip off a stance. Climbers find them invaluable when setting up anchors or shifting positions on a crowded belay station.

- Pros: Quick length changes, dynamic rope, comfortable for stance adjustments.
- Cons: Slightly more expensive than a basic sling or static lanyard.

### Single vs double lanyards

A single lanyard has just one arm, which works fine if you always stay clipped into a single anchor. A double lanyard, by contrast, has two arms, making it easier to move between anchor points without ever fully unclipping. This is helpful for multi-pitch routes or complex abseils.

- Single lanyard: Simpler, lighter, fewer tangles.
- Double lanyard: Offers a second arm to extend your rappel device or attach to a second anchor, giving you extra freedom.

## Work with standard slings

Slings are looped pieces of webbing or cord, and they're a staple in any climber's rack. They range from sewn Dyneema slings to rope-style cord. You'll see them used for anchor-building, extending protection, or bridging a short gap during a climb. Slings can also serve as a personal anchor, though typically with some limitations compared to a dedicated dynamic lanyard.

### Chain style PAS

A popular product for anchor attachments is the chain style Personal Anchor System (PAS). Made of interconnected loops of high-strength material like Dyneema, a chain PAS can be clipped at various lengths. It's incredibly versatile, allowing you to create equalised anchors quickly or extend your belay device without retying knots.

- Strength: While convenient, chain-style PAS is usually static, meaning it won't absorb much force in a fall. A short slip can still create high shock loads.
- Tip: Some climbers prefer a rope-based PAS because it can handle dynamic forces better than Dyneema loops.

### Rope lanyards vs static webbing

Rope lanyards, especially adjustable ones like the Petzl Connect Adjust, are becoming more popular due to their dynamic nature. In a factor-two fall scenario, rope lanyards stand a better chance of preventing excessive force on the anchor or your harness. A static sling can be used as a backup or to build anchors, but it isn't your best friend if there's a possibility of falling onto it from above the anchor point.

### Custom cord setups

Using cord (5 to 8mm) tied in a loop with a double fisherman's knot has been a classic approach. This loop can serve multiple roles: a friction hitch backup for rappelling, a basic tether at a belay, or an emergency sling in rescue scenarios. It's cheaper than a specialised lanyard and easy to replace.

- Pros: Versatile, cost-effective, can be cut to any desired length.
- Cons: Might not be CE certified if you simply buy cord separately, and the dynamic properties may vary.

## Follow inspection steps

Your gear is your lifeline, so you want to keep it in prime condition. Regular inspections are vital, especially when you subject your lanyard or sling to intense outdoor use.

### Daily check routine

Before each outing, run through a quick list:

1. Inspect stitching: Check if any thread is loose or damaged on sewn slings or lanyards.
2. Examine webbing or rope: Look for frayed fibres, cuts, glaze marks from heat, or discoloration indicating sun damage.
3. Test connectors: Carabiners, snaphooks, or D-rings must have gates in good shape-no cracks, and they must spring back properly.
4. Confirm hardware ratings: If you see WLL or MBS markings rubbed off or unreadable, think about replacing the item.
5. Feel for stiffness: Rope-based lanyards might stiffen after repeated falls or dirt exposure, so gently flex them to spot damage.

### Retirement guidelines

Even with minimal use, most sling manufacturers recommend you replace slings after around 10 years. Realistically, if you're abseiling every weekend or took a big fall on your gear, you should retire it sooner. A severe fall can compromise internal fibres, so if you have any doubts about its condition, it's better to retire it. This principle also applies to dynamic rope lanyards and chain style PAS devices.

Use this rough timeline as a guide:

- 2-5 years: If you use it moderately and see mild wear.
- Immediately: If it's been in a major fall or shows significant damage.
- 10 years: Maximum recommended shelf life, even if unused.

## Wrap up your safety

There's a lot to consider when you're picking and maintaining lanyards, slings, or any piece of abseiling gear. Still, a sensible routine helps ensure you're always prepared when you head for the cliff's edge. Below are some final pointers to tie it all together.

- Use lanyards that match your climbing style: Recreational abseilers or novices might opt for dynamic rope-based lanyards, while industrial rope access technicians often rely on shock-absorbing models.
- Check for certifications: CE markings indicate tested reliability. OSHA guidelines specify strength, corrosion resistance, and gate requirements for connectors.
- Back it up: If you use a chain style PAS, consider adding a short rope-based tether for additional absorption. Or keep a double lanyard so you can stay clipped in twice as you move around an anchor station.
- Make a rescue plan: Whenever you're working at height, especially with gear like Self-Retracting Lifelines (SRLs), have a plan in place if something goes wrong.
- Pair lanyards with other essentials: Good gloves protect against rope burns. A backup friction hitch or autoblock can give you an extra layer of safety during descents.

### FAQ's

**Q. What's the difference between WLL and MBS?**  
A. WLL is the maximum safe working load, often on hardware like rapides; MBS is the breaking strength for gear like lanyards, showing the force at which it will fail.

**Q. Should I choose dynamic or static lanyards for abseiling?**  
A. Dynamic lanyards absorb force and are safer for potential falls; static lanyards are better for stable positioning or anchor building.

**Q. Can a sling replace a lanyard?**  
A. Yes, but most slings are static and won't absorb much shock in a fall. They're best for anchors or short, low-risk attachments.

**Q. How often should lanyards or slings be replaced?**  
A. Replace immediately if damaged or after a major fall; typically every 2-5 years with regular use, or 10 years max if unused.

**Q. What's the safest option for moving between anchors?**  
A. A double lanyard or a chain-style PAS lets you stay clipped to at least one point while moving between anchors.

## Final thoughts

As you gear up for abseiling adventures, rappelling lanyards deserve your close attention. They're not just accessories, but safety devices that help keep you connected to that anchor high above the ground. Take time to understand the ratings, choose a dynamic or static option (or both) based on your needs, and stay on top of inspections. Retirement and replacement can sometimes feel wasteful, but trust me, your life is worth the investment.

The best approach is to begin with a style that makes sense for your usual routes. If you're new, a dynamic, adjustable product like the Petzl Connect Adjust or Camp Swing takes much of the guesswork out, giving you a secure, comfy attachment. Experienced climbers might combine a chain style PAS for quick anchor setups with a short dynamic tether for stronger protection. Regardless, treat these tools with care, check them before each climb, and replace them when they show signs of wear.

Remember, no single piece of gear is a guarantee. You're stacking layers of safety, from your harness and rope to your slings, carabiners, and beyond. When everything works together, that moment of stepping backward off the edge feels a lot more confident-which is exactly how you want it to be. Enjoy your descents, and stay safe out there!
