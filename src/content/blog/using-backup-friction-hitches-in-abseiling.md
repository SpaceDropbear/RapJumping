## TL;DR

- A prusik backup acts as a hands-free safety brake if your brake hand slips during abseiling.
- Use ~1.5 m of 6 mm cord tied in a loop with a double fisherman's knot.
- Place the hitch below the descender for easier control and release.
- Test friction before committing-add wraps if it slips, reduce wraps if it jams.
- Practise use and rescue scenarios on easy ground before big descents.

---

If you love the thrill of abseiling but sometimes feel a twinge of worry about losing your grip or your brake hand, a prusik backup can ease those nerves. This simple friction hitch is a safety net that helps you halt your descent if things go sideways. Today, you'll learn exactly how to set up a prusik hitch for abseiling, test it so you know it works, and understand why it's worth adding to your routine. Ready to boost your peace of mind before your next big drop? Let's dive in.

## Gather your essential gear

Before you begin, round up the basics so you can tie and test your friction hitch properly. Think of this as a mini checklist, making sure you've covered every angle to keep your abseiling safe and smooth.

1. Rope and harness
   - Choose a rope suitable for your abseil (single or double).
   - Make sure your harness is in good condition, with the belay loop fully intact.
2. Prusik cord
   - Aim for around 1.5 metres of cord, typically 6 mm in diameter.
   - Check that the cord diameter contrasts well with your main rope. If the rope is too similar in diameter, your prusik may not bite properly.
3. Locking carabiners
   - Carry two or three reliable locking carabiners. You'll need at least one to attach your prusik hitch to your harness, and another for your abseil device.
4. Abseil device
   - A standard belay or descender device, such as an ATC (Air Traffic Controller) or figure-eight, works well.
   - Double-check that the device is designed for the rope thickness you're using.
5. Personal anchor sling (optional)
   - This can help you secure yourself to an anchor while setting up your gear, especially when you're on an exposed ledge.

## Prepare your abseil setup

The next step is setting up your abseil device in a way that leaves room for your prusik backup. One approach many people use is an “extended abseil,” which simply means putting a short sling between your harness and your descender. This arrangement gives you enough space below the device to tie your hitch while keeping both hands free when needed.

1. Attach a short sling to your harness's belay loop
   - Clip one end of the sling to your belay loop with a locking carabiner.
   - Attach your descender to the other end of the sling with another locking carabiner.
2. Thread your rope through the descender
   - Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the device you use.
   - Lock your carabiner once the rope is threaded.
3. Secure yourself to the anchor (if available)
   - Use a personal anchor sling or a cow's tail.
   - This gives you more confidence while you get ready, especially when the abseil stance is cramped or exposed.

You might ask, “Why not just attach the abseil device to my harness without extending it?” Extending the abseil device frees up the space below, which is ideal for tying your prusik hitch so it can grab the rope quickly if you lose your brake hand.

## Tie the prusik hitch below your device

Placing your prusik backup below the descender is often recommended because it only has to hold the same load as your brake hand. In contrast, putting it above means it must carry your entire body weight when it engages, which can be difficult to release. Below, you'll find the steps to tie the prusik hitch, also known as a self-belay or third-hand.

1. Form the prusik loop
   - Take your 1.5-metre cord and tie the ends together with a double fisherman's knot.
   - Make sure this loop is snug but not overly tight. You'll want a bit of slack so you can easily wrap it around the main rope.
2. Wrap the cord around your rope
   - With the rope weighted by your abseil device above, wrap the loop around the rope two to four times.
   - More wraps mean more friction, especially with thinner cords, wet ropes, or heavier loads.
3. Clip the prusik loop to your harness
   - Attach the bottom of the loop to your harness's belay loop (or a dedicated tie-in point) using a locking carabiner.
   - Give it a slight tug to confirm everything is properly loaded and not twisted.
4. Slide the prusik hitch to test friction
   - Slide the hitch up and down the rope with your hand. When there's no weight on it, it should move easily.
   - Let go and see if it grips. If it slips, add another wrap.

Remember, never clip the prusik loop to your leg loop. If you do, you risk tipping upside down when the hitch locks off. By clipping into your belay loop, you keep yourself upright if the hitch engages.

## Test your prusik friction

Before you commit to abseiling, give your new setup a quick test. Think of it as a simple habit that might save your life someday.

1. Weight the rope gently
   - Lean back slightly and let your device take some weight.
   - Keep one hand on the brake rope as usual, but let the prusik hitch share a bit of the load.
2. Simulate a slip
   - Carefully ease off your brake hand pressure.
   - Watch the prusik. It should grip firmly and stop you from sliding.
3. Check release
   - Once it's weighted, see how easily you can loosen the hitch. Some friction knots, like a prusik, can be harder to free when fully loaded.
   - If it's too tough to release, consider fewer wraps or switching to an autoblock or klemheist that's easier to manage under load.
4. Practise partial descents
   - Lower yourself a short distance, then stop with the prusik.
   - Get a feel for how to lock your hitch and how to ease it open again.

If everything feels smooth, you're set to abseil with a prusik-based safety net. If the prusik is temperamental, experiment with minor tweaks. Sometimes, an extra wrap or a slightly different cord thickness can make all the difference.

## Abseil with control and confidence

Now it's time to descend. Here's where your prusik backup becomes a real hero. When you rely on friction hitches to catch you if your brake hand slips, you get more mental bandwidth for other tasks: checking the rope, planning your next move, or dealing with tricky transitions.

- Manage your brake hand
  - Keep it below the device, gently guiding the rope as you move.
  - If you ever need to stop and fiddle with gear, just let the prusik engage.
- Keep an eye on rope ends
  - If your rope is not knotted at the ends, you could slip off the rope entirely. Make sure you tie a stopper knot in each end or confirm you have enough rope for the full descent.
- Maintain situational awareness
  - Watch out for ledges, loose rocks, or rope tangles.
  - If you come across unexpected terrain, you can pause, let the prusik hold you, and re-evaluate.
- Use [abseiling safety](/abseiling-safety-and-emergency-procedures-guide/) tips
  - A prusik backup isn't your only precaution. Double-check anchors, wear a helmet, and communicate with your partner if you're descending in a team.

## Know your other friction hitch options

Although a prusik hitch is a popular choice, you can also consider autoblock or klemheist. Each friction hitch offers safety benefits, but they differ in friction strength and how easily they can be released under load.

| Friction Hitch | Friction Strength | Ease of Release When Weighted | Recommended Cord/Webbing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prusik | High | Hard to loosen when fully weighted | Cord (6 mm typical) |
| Autoblock | Medium | Generally easier to loosen, fewer wraps | Cord or sling |
| Klemheist | High, unidirectional only | Fairly manageable, but direction matters | Can be used with cord or webbing |

- Autoblock
  - Often takes fewer wraps (3-4) around the rope.
  - Might slip more if the rope is icy or very thin, so test carefully.
  - Loosens more easily, so it's a favourite if you expect to stop mid-descent.
- Klemheist
  - Similar friction to a prusik if wrapped enough times.
  - Only grips effectively in one direction, which can be perfect for abseiling but less flexible for multi-direction climbing tasks.

No matter which friction hitch you choose, the goal is the same: catch your fall if the brake hand fails. All these methods are considered a type of “third hand” that you can rely on when unexpected slips occur.

## Expand your hitch repertoire

Beyond the prusik, autoblock and klemheist, three more hitches earn a place in your toolkit - each with a different grip-and-release personality:

- **Blake's Hitch** works with a single cord end and tolerates ropes of similar diameter: form about four wraps upward around the rope, bring the cord back down through the top two wraps, and set. A favourite for quick descents or as an emergency stand-in.
- **Distel Hitch** offers the smoothest release for its grip: loop behind the rope, three to four upward wraps, exit between the topmost wrap and the rest, dress and tighten. Consistent across varied rope diameters.
- **Farrimond friction hitch** is the quick-release option - a prusik-style wrap with an extra slip-loop pass that frees with a tug on the trigger loop. Treat it as a utility hitch (guy lines, haul control), **not** a primary life-support backup.

Two gear notes worth knowing. Stitched-eye hitch cords are increasingly the standard - the compact eye keeps the stiff section short so the hitch sits neatly on the rope. And material matters: nylon, polyester or heat-resistant aramids (e.g., Technora) grip well, while Dyneema is slick with a low melting point - avoid it for friction hitches.

**Bottom line: master the prusik first, then add Blake's for one-ended versatility and the Distel for smooth release - and keep the Farrimond for utility work only.**

## Troubleshoot common pitfalls

Even with a solid prusik backup, some issues might pop up. Here's how to tackle them.

- Prusik hitch won't grip
  - Possible cause: Cord diameter is too close to rope diameter.
  - Fix: Use a slightly thinner prusik cord or add more wraps.
- Prusik jammed tight
  - Possible cause: Fully weighted hitch is tough to release.
  - Fix: Gently stand up on one foot or use a small sling to shift weight off the knot so you can free it.
- Rope tangles
  - Possible cause: Loose ends, twisted harness attachments.
  - Fix: Flake your rope to remove twists and keep brake rope tidy during your descent.
- Accidental upside-down lockoff
  - Possible cause: Backup hitch attached to the leg loop, pulling you upside down.
  - Fix: Always clip the friction hitch to your belay loop or tie-in points on the waist portion of your harness.

## Practise rescue skills

What if you get stuck halfway down, or if you need to help a partner in trouble? Having a prusik (or two) on your harness can be a real life-saver.

- Ascending the rope
  - Tie one prusik on your rope, then use it as a foot loop.
  - Tie another prusik above that for your harness.
  - Move one prusik up, then step up and move the other. This is the basis of a simple rope ascent.
- Escaping the system
  - If you need to free your hands to help someone else, let your backup friction hitch hold you safely in place.
  - This is particularly helpful if you're assisting a partner who's stuck or checking knots and gear mid-rappel.
- Tandem abseils or heavy loads
  - When descending with someone else or carrying a hefty pack, your prusik can help control extra weight.
  - Rappel devices are typically rated for single-person loads, so an additional friction hitch can be a vital line of defence.

By keeping a prusik cord handy, you're better prepared for those unexpected moments. And since the cord weighs next to nothing, there's no big reason to leave it behind.

## Stay mindful of time and energy

One of the arguments people make against using a prusik backup is that it takes extra time to set up. Realistically, it might only add a minute or two to your total rigging. Compare that small investment to the potential risks of abseiling without a backup-most would agree it's time well spent.

- Quick rigging tips
  - Pre-cut your cord to the right length.
  - Keep your prusik cord accessible on your harness gear loop, so it's not buried under other kit.
- Myth busting
  - “It slows me down.” In truth, once you've practised, tying a friction hitch becomes second nature.
  - “It's not really necessary.” Statistics show that abseiling accidents can be serious or fatal. A simple friction hitch has saved many climbers from lost brake-hand mishaps.

## Step-by-step recap for clarity

To ensure you have a quick reference guide, here's a summary of how to set up and use your prusik hitch:

1. Attach and extend your abseil device from your harness's belay loop.
2. Tie your prusik loop with a double fisherman's knot (about 1.5 metres of 6 mm cord).
3. Wrap the cord around your rope 2-4 times, forming a friction hitch below your descender.
4. Clip the prusik hitch to your harness belay loop with a locking carabiner.
5. Test the hitch by gently leaning back and letting it grip the rope.
6. Abseil normally, using your brake hand. If it slips, your prusik backup will hold you in place.
7. Practise releasing the hitch under load by shifting your weight off the prusik.

## Check your overall abseiling safety

A prusik backup is just one part of a larger picture. Safe abseiling involves a handful of procedures and constant vigilance. If you'd like more guidance on anchor checks, rope inspection, or emergency planning, you might want to explore abseiling safety. It's all about covering your bases so your descent goes smoothly-even if surprises pop up.

- Inspect your gear
  - Examine your harness for wear on the belay loop and webbing.
  - Check carabiners for dents or gate malfunctions.
  - Look for fuzziness or cuts in your rope or prusik cord.
- Communicate with your climbing partner
  - Confirm who's going first, who's last, and what the ideal signal is if you need to stop.
  - Make sure they're ready to spot you or assist if something heads south.
- Plan for odd conditions
  - Wet ropes can reduce friction, so extra wraps on the prusik might be needed.
  - Cold hands can lose grip faster, emphasising the importance of that backup friction hitch.
  - Heavy packs alter your centre of gravity, making it harder to maintain control if you slip.

## Practice makes perfect

Like any rope technique, a prusik hitch is only useful if you're comfortable with it. It helps to train on short, controlled descents before committing to a big face.

- Start on easy ground
  - Practise on a short, easy abseil where your feet can still touch the ground if anything goes wrong.
  - Once you trust your hitch, move on to bigger challenges.
- Drill rescue scenarios
  - Pretend your main device fails and rely solely on the prusik for a few seconds. That boosts your confidence in real-life conditions.
  - Practise freeing a jammed prusik by standing on a foot loop. The smoother you are at these tasks, the less panic you'll feel if something ever does happen.
- Fun day out
  - Turn your practice session into something enjoyable with friends. It's often less nerve-wracking to learn with a supportive group around you.

### FAQ's

**Q. What is a prusik backup in abseiling?**  
A. It's a friction hitch tied on the rope below your descender that grips automatically if you lose brake-hand control.

**Q. Why place the prusik hitch below the descender?**  
A. Below the device, the prusik only holds brake-hand load, making it easier to release than if it's above.

**Q. How long should the prusik cord be?**  
A. Around 1.5 m of 6 mm cord is common, tied into a closed loop with a double fisherman's knot.

**Q. What if my prusik won't grip?**  
A. Use a thinner cord relative to your main rope or add extra wraps for more friction.

**Q. Are there alternatives to the prusik hitch?**  
A. Yes-autoblock and klemheist knots are also popular, each with different grip and release characteristics. Blake's and Distel hitches suit specific rope setups.

**Q. How many wraps do I need?**  
A. Start with 3 wraps on dry, mid-diameter ropes; add a wrap for skinny, wet or icy ropes until it grips reliably.

**Q. Nylon, aramid, or Dyneema for hitch cord?**  
A. Nylon/polyester or heat-resistant aramids (e.g., Technora) grip well. Dyneema is slick with a low melting point-avoid it for friction hitches.

**Q. Can I use the Farrimond hitch for abseiling backup?**  
A. No-treat it as a utility/quick-release hitch for guys or haul control. Use prusik, klemheist or Distel for life-support backups.
