The Glass House Mountains are one of the most recognisable skylines in Queensland: a scatter of sheer-sided volcanic peaks rising abruptly out of the flat coastal plain in the Sunshine Coast hinterland, about an hour north of Brisbane. For climbers, they have long held a particular fascination, steep, exposed rock on genuine mountain features rather than a roadside crag. But the Glass House Mountains are also a place where the honest, up-to-date answer to "can I climb it?" depends entirely on which peak you mean and what QPWS has published this week.

That is the whole point of this guide. The headline fact every climber needs first: Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck) has been closed to climbing and to public access at the base of its cliffs since 1999, and that closure remains in place. Other peaks are open to experienced parties but carry their own hazards and are subject to temporary closures. Rock climbing shares its DNA with [rap jumping](/) and abseiling. All three depend on sound anchors, careful rope-work and a calm head at height. So if these peaks appeal but you are new to rope systems, a guided rap jumping session is a controlled, well-supervised way to get comfortable on rope before you ever tie in on exposed volcanic rock.

Below we cover the peaks and their geology, the cultural significance of this place, a plain "what's open / what's not" rundown, the general style of climbing here, how to get there, the best season, and the gear-and-safety mindset this terrain demands. Throughout, treat this article as a starting point and QPWS park alerts as the source of truth: access here genuinely changes, and only the land manager can tell you the current position.

## About the peaks: rock, geology and character

The Glass House Mountains are a cluster of volcanic plugs (the eroded cores of ancient volcanoes) standing in and around Glass House Mountains National Park in the Sunshine Coast hinterland, near the towns of Beerburrum and Beerwah, roughly 70km north of Brisbane. The rock is predominantly trachyte and rhyolite, hard volcanic stone that has weathered into the steep, columnar faces and abrupt summits that give the range its distinctive profile. The park is managed by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service (QPWS), part of the Department of the Environment, Tourism, Science and Innovation.

The best known peaks include **Mount Beerwah**, the highest at around 556 metres; **Mount Tibrogargan**, with its bold, brooding face; **Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck)**, the slender, dramatic spire; **Mount Ngungun**; and **Mount Beerburrum**, among others. Each is a discrete landform rather than part of a continuous ridge, which is part of what makes the range so striking, and why access and closures have to be considered peak by peak rather than as a single "area".

For climbers, the appeal is the scale and exposure: these are real mountain features with multi-pitch potential, not a short single-pitch cliff. The flip side is that volcanic plugs like these are known for variable rock quality. Loose blocks, friable sections and rockfall are documented, well-understood hazards here. Indeed, rockfall risk is the central reason Mount Coonowrin is closed. This is committing terrain that rewards experience, sound judgement and conservative decision-making far more than it rewards ambition.

## Cultural significance: Jinibara and Kabi Kabi (Gubbi Gubbi) country

Before anything else, it is important to say plainly that the Glass House Mountains are a place of deep and continuing cultural and spiritual significance to the Jinibara and Kabi Kabi (Gubbi Gubbi) peoples, the Traditional Owners and custodians of this country. The peaks hold profound meaning in the living cultural landscape of the region, and that significance is not a historical footnote. It is present, and it matters to how the mountains are cared for and visited today.

For climbers, respecting that significance is part of visiting responsibly. It means recognising that some places and some peaks may carry cultural restrictions or sensitivities that sit alongside the practical access rules, that the decision to climb a peak is not purely a recreational one, and that the closure of Mount Coonowrin is grounded in cultural considerations as well as geological ones. QPWS manages these peaks in a context that includes Traditional Owner interests, and the respectful default is to follow official access guidance, stay on formed tracks and established routes, and treat the mountains as the significant places they are.

## What's open and what's not

This is the section climbers come here for, and the honest short version is: **it depends on the peak, and it can change, so verify against QPWS park alerts before you travel.** With that caveat front and centre, here is the general picture as it stands.

### Mount Coonowrin (Crookneck): CLOSED

Mount Coonowrin has been a declared restricted access area since 1999, and it is closed to rock climbing and to public access at the base of the cliffs. The closure followed a geological assessment that found a high-to-very-high risk of rockfall from the cliffs around the peak, with a corresponding risk to anyone accessing the area; it also reflects the cultural significance of the peak. This is not a grey area and not a "climb at your own risk" situation. The base trail and climbing are closed, the closure remains in place, and QPWS publishes a dedicated Mount Coonowrin restricted access page setting this out. Do not climb Coonowrin.

### Mount Beerwah: open, with a closure history

Mount Beerwah, the highest peak, is generally open to suitably experienced and well-prepared parties, but it carries a cautionary history worth knowing. Its summit walking route was closed in 2009 after a rock collapse and reopened in January 2016. That history is a useful reminder that these are active, weathering rock faces where conditions genuinely change, and that even an "open" peak can be temporarily closed for safety, weather, fire or maintenance at any time.

### Tibrogargan, Ngungun and other peaks: climbing exists, check current status

Climbing and multi-pitch routes exist on peaks such as Tibrogargan, Beerwah and Ngungun. Where a peak is open, it is open to competent, self-reliant climbers who can manage exposure and loose rock. It is not beginner terrain. Because any peak can be subject to a temporary closure, and because access arrangements are the land manager's to set, always check the QPWS park alerts for Glass House Mountains National Park immediately before a trip.

### Always check QPWS park alerts

Access here is not static. On top of the standing Coonowrin closure, individual peaks and tracks are periodically closed for weather, bushfire, storm damage, rockfall or maintenance. There has also been public discussion in recent years about the future of summit access across the range; whatever you may read elsewhere, the single authoritative, checkable source of truth is QPWS, the Glass House Mountains National Park pages and the official park alerts at parks.qld.gov.au. Check them before you drive up, and follow whatever they say on the day.

## Climbing styles and grades

Climbing in the Glass House Mountains is, in general terms, exposed multi-pitch and single-pitch climbing on steep volcanic rock, a mountain-feature experience rather than a manicured crag. Historically the range has seen traditional ("trad") climbing, where the leader places their own removable protection into the rock rather than clipping fixed bolts, as well as scrambling and committing summit routes. This is terrain where route-finding, rope management on multi-pitch ground and the ability to build sound anchors matter as much as pure climbing difficulty.

Grades in Australia follow the Ewbank system, a single open-ended number that reflects a route's overall difficulty, sustainedness and seriousness rather than just its hardest single move. Because we have not independently verified individual route names, grades or route counts on these peaks from an authoritative source, we have deliberately not listed any here. That is a considered choice, not an omission: on terrain with real loose-rock and access considerations, the responsible approach is to consult a current, area-specific route guide and up-to-date local information when you are genuinely planning a climb, rather than relying on figures in a general article. For a full explanation of the Ewbank scale and how it compares with overseas systems, see our companion guide, [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/).

What the Glass House Mountains reward is mountaineering judgement: assessing rock quality, moving efficiently on exposed ground, protecting loose sections sensibly, and knowing when to back off. If your background is bolted sport climbing at the gym or a roadside crag, treat these peaks as a genuine step up in commitment and self-reliance, closer in character to the trad and multi-pitch terrain we describe at [Frog Buttress](/frog-buttress-rock-climbing-qlds-world-class-rhyolite-crag/) than to a clip-up sport wall.

## Getting there and access

Glass House Mountains National Park sits in the Sunshine Coast hinterland, around 70km north of Brisbane and readily reached off the Bruce Highway via the townships of Glass House Mountains, Beerburrum and Beerwah. The park is made up of separate sections around the individual peaks, each with its own car parks and tracks, so it pays to know which peak you are heading to and to check its specific access page before setting out.

Because this is a live, managed conservation area with real safety and cultural considerations, always check current conditions and access before you travel:

- QPWS park alerts for Glass House Mountains National Park, for any temporary closures, track works, rockfall notices or fire-related restrictions on the peak you are visiting.

- The QPWS Mount Coonowrin restricted access page, which confirms Coonowrin remains closed to climbing and base access.

- Bureau of Meteorology forecasts and current bushfire warnings for the Sunshine Coast area. Hot conditions and storms materially change the risk on exposed rock.

- Whether any peak-specific access or seasonal restriction applies before you commit to a route.

The Australian Climbing Association Queensland (ACAQ) is the recognised peak body and consultative entity for climbing access matters in the state, and works with QPWS and other land managers on issues affecting areas like the Glass House Mountains. Supporting or joining your state's Australian Climbing Association is one of the most practical things a climber can do to help keep access sustainable and to stay informed about the state of play.

## Best season and conditions

Like most South East Queensland climbing, the Glass House Mountains are at their best in the cooler, drier months, broadly autumn through to spring. The region's subtropical summer brings heat, high humidity and afternoon storms, all of which make committing climbs on exposed volcanic rock less pleasant and more hazardous, and storm activity raises the risk of loose or wet rock. Winter tends to offer the most stable, comfortable conditions for time spent high on a peak.

Whatever the season, avoid these peaks in the wet. Volcanic rock loses friction when damp, wet conditions increase the chance of rockfall and slips on already-loose ground, and storms can roll in quickly. Combine a good forecast with a current check of QPWS park alerts, and be prepared to change your plans. On this kind of terrain, walking away from marginal conditions is the experienced call, not the timid one.

## Gear and safety in the Glass House Mountains

Climbing in the Glass House Mountains is committing, exposed and (crucially) takes place on rock where loose blocks and rockfall are a real, documented hazard. That single fact should shape every decision here. This is not terrain for teaching yourself, and it is not a beginner venue. If you are new to climbing, or new to multi-pitch and trad, the safest and fastest way to build real competency is through experienced mentorship or structured instruction from a qualified guide, ideally over multiple outings, before you take on exposed volcanic peaks.

Sensible precautions on this kind of terrain include:

- **Always wear a helmet.** On loose volcanic rock with genuine rockfall potential, head protection is not optional, for you and for anyone below.

- **Manage loose rock actively.** Test holds, avoid dislodging blocks onto climbers or walkers below, and choose belays that keep the party out of the fall line where you can.

- **Carry appropriate protection and anchor-building gear** for exposed multi-pitch ground, and the skills to place and assess it under pressure.

- **Check the forecast and the park alerts,** carry enough water for hot, exposed conditions, and be honest about turning back if the rock is wet, the weather is deteriorating or conditions feel marginal.

As with any climbing hardware used in Australia, look for recognised certification: carabiners to EN 12275 (and/or the equivalent UIAA standard), harnesses to EN 12277, dynamic ropes to EN 892, and helmets to EN 12492. Our [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/) covers these standards, and general gear and safety fundamentals, in more depth. Finally, respect the area's access rules and cultural significance: never enter the closed Mount Coonowrin area, stay on formed tracks and established routes, and follow QPWS guidance on the day.

## Learning the systems before an exposed peak

The Glass House Mountains are committing, exposed and prone to loose rock, and closures are part of the picture. This is terrain that rewards years of judgement. A guided [rap jumping](/) or abseiling session with a qualified operator is where that judgement starts, with certified equipment and every system checked by an instructor. Build the fundamentals there, and check QPWS alerts before you go anywhere near these peaks.
