Natural light levels can shift dramatically on a mountain, but when you're performing a tandem abseil, you want to ensure every detail of your setup is strong and visible. This technique, which involves two people descending on the same device, often comes into play if you have an injured partner or need an efficient way to get down together. Though it can look daunting, tandem abseiling is perfectly manageable once you understand the gear, anchor-building strategies, and step-by-step method. Below, you'll find a complete guide on how to pull it off safely and confidently.

## Understand tandem abseiling

Tandem abseiling simply means you and a partner share one belay device and descend together. It's commonly used if a fellow climber is incapacitated, or you both want to save time on a multi-pitch route. Because this method involves two sets of hands and feet, there's an added layer of complexity.

Abseiling itself can be risky, and different studies have reported that about 25% of climbing deaths occur during the descent stage, largely due to anchor failures or rope mishandling. Yet, tandem abseiling doesn't have to be more dangerous than a typical descent. What matters is your preparation and consistent use of safety backups. Wondering if you should try it? If you're comfortable with standard abseiling techniques, can build a reliable anchor, and have practised with your partner on solid ground, you're in a good position to explore this intermediate skill.

## Gather your gear

Before you step to the edge, you want to make sure your gear is up to scratch. Solid equipment prevents many of the common mishaps that can occur when both climbers rely on a single system.

- Rope: A dynamic or semi-static rope suitable for abseiling. Check the length to ensure it's long enough for your planned descent. Some people use the Reepschnur method with a thin cord to retrieve the main rope afterward, but be prepared for snags.
- Belay device: Choose one that offers additional friction for two-person loads. Typically, a tube-style device or guide-mode device with bigger braking capability works well.
- Harnesses: Each person has a properly fitted climbing harness. Inspect for any wear or damage.
- Slings: You'll need at least two shoulder-length slings. One is girth-hitched through the belay loop of the lead abseiler, and the other is doubled and clipped through the second abseiler's harness.
- Screwgate carabiners: At least two locked carabiners go into the belay device to attach each climber. Have extras for backups and attaching prusiks.
- Prusik cord: A shorter length of friction cord (often 5-6mm in diameter) to create a ‘third hand' backup under your belay device.
- Helmet: Falling rocks, gear, or ice can be a hazard, so always wear a helmet.

If you're still sharpening basic rope-handling or anchor-building skills, consider reviewing additional [abseiling techniques](/mastering-abseiling-techniques-and-rope-skills/) before attempting two-person descents.

## Prepare the anchor

Anchors are everything in climbing and abseiling, and a tandem abseil anchor should be doubly secure. Always set up redundancy so you have at least two bombproof points. If you're relying on natural protection (like trees or boulders), thoroughly evaluate the surface for cracks, rot, and stability. For bolted routes, inspect bolts for rust or looseness.

1. Identify strong points. Pick your anchor based on tested and true pieces of gear or robust rock features.
2. Equalise. Use a sling or cordelette to distribute the load across each anchor point.
3. Double-check friction angles. A wide angle in your anchor arms can weaken the setup.
4. Inspect everything. Tug, weigh, and ask yourself whether you trust your anchor for two climbers.

A good anchor is your lifeline, especially when you're supporting someone else's entire weight along with your own.

## Perform the setup

Once your anchor is ready, it's time for the main event: attaching yourself and your partner to the belay device in tandem. Let's break it down step by step, so you can follow a clear routine.

1. Position yourselves. The lead abseiler is the person who manages the belay device. Stand on a stable ledge where both climbers have enough room to move around safely.
2. Attach the belay device. Girth-hitch a shoulder-length sling through the lead abseiler's belay loop. Clip the belay device onto it with a locking carabiner. Verify the carabiner is locked before anything else.
3. Clip in your rope. Pass the rope through your belay device as you normally would, ensuring the brake strand is on the correct side. The device should be oriented so it will give maximum friction to handle both weights.
4. Add the second climber. The second person uses a doubled sling through their harness tie-in points. Clip this sling into the same belay device with a separate locking carabiner. This keeps you slightly staggered, preventing you from tangling.
5. Check friction. With two people on one device, you generally need additional friction. Ensure your belay device handles the rope smoothly, but with enough stopping power to control the descent.
6. Buddy check. Is the anchor sound? Carabiners locked? Rope ends secured? Confirm every detail one final time.

## Use a backup

There's a reason pro climbers carry a prusik cord on every outing. A third hand below your belay device can arrest your fall if you lose grip. You might have seen older techniques where a prusik is placed above the belay device, but many climbers have moved away from that method because it can end up stuck out of reach.

- Create an autoblock: Tie a short prusik or an autoblock around the brake rope below your belay device, clipping the free end of the prusik cord into your harness leg loop with a screwgate.
- Test the lock-off: Gently pull your hand away from the brake strand (lowering it slowly) to see if the friction hitch catches. This step is key to confirming your backup's reliability.
- Never skip the backup: A tandem abseil means more weight and more potential risk if something goes wrong. The backup adds a crucial layer of protection.

## Handle an injured partner

A frequent scenario for using a tandem abseil is when your partner can't descend alone. If they're hurt-or just not fully mobile-you'll need extra caution to get them down safely.

1. Pre-attach a clip-in. Before you leave each station, attach your partner to the anchor with a releasable clip-in, like a cord tied with a munter-mule-overhand knot. Back it up with a sling. This way, if anything goes awry, you can lower or free them more easily.
2. Secure them at each station. Every time you reach a new anchor, repeat the process of clipping your injured partner in. This prevents accidental slips and shares the load.
3. Set up a chest harness. If necessary, use a double-length sling to keep your partner upright. Make an overhand knot in the middle of the sling, put their arms through each loop, and clip the ends around the abseil rope. This stops them from tipping backward or forward.
4. Descend carefully. Take it slow and communicate. If your partner is incapacitated, they might not be able to shift their weight as effectively. Keep an eye on whether the sling holds them in a comfortable position.

When you're working with an injured person, you have to factor in the time needed at each station to re-check knots, slings, and rope angles. Rushing can lead to simple oversights with serious consequences.

## Avoid common mistakes

Heading down a vertical face together creates new opportunities for slip-ups, even if you're experienced. Most tandem abseil mishaps can be traced to human error, so forming good habits keeps you both out of trouble.

- Not securing rope ends. One of the biggest pitfalls is descending off the end of your rope. Always tie knots in the ends or make sure your rope is long enough.
- Clipping the device incorrectly. With two slings and multiple carabiners attached to one device, it's easy to tangle lines. Take a moment to trace your setup visually.
- Skipping the final check. Distractions are a major risk factor. Make it a habit for both climbers to do a quick verbal run-through: “Anchors? Belay device locked? Prusik in place? Rope ends knotted?”
- Neglecting friction. Overloading a minimal belay device can lead to rope slippage. If you notice it's tough to brake, add an extra twist in the rope or use a belay device known for high friction.
- Improper rope retrieval. If you try advanced methods like the Reepschnur technique, be prepared that knots or carabiners can jam when you pull the rope. Keep a calm head if you need to climb back up a bit to free it.

Staying systematic is the name of the game. Develop a routine-like muscle memory-that you follow for every descent.

### FAQs

**Q. Is tandem abseiling safe?**  
A. As safe as your systems. Use a redundant anchor, high-friction device, and a prusik/autoblock backup every time.

**Q. Who should control the device?**  
A. The more experienced/stronger climber. They manage braking, pace, and comms.

**Q. Tube or guide-mode device?**  
A. Either works if it provides enough friction for two loads. Test before committing; add friction (extra turns) if needed.

**Q. Dynamic or static rope?**  
A. Semi-static/static is preferred for descents; dynamic can work but will feel bouncy with two people.

**Q. Where does the prusik go-above or below?**  
A. Below the device on the brake strand (autoblock style) so it remains reachable and won't jam out of reach.

**Q. How do we prevent going off the rope ends?**  
A. Knot both rope ends and confirm rope length reaches the next stance.

**Q. What if our weights differ a lot?**  
A. Put the heavier climber as the device controller, add friction, and keep the lighter climber slightly below/staggered on their sling.

**Q. How do I keep an injured partner upright?**  
A. Use a makeshift chest harness (double sling with mid-knot) clipped to the abseil rope to stop tipping.

**Q. What checks do we do before leaving the anchor?**  
A. “Anchors equalised? Carabiners locked? Device oriented? Prusik on? Rope ends knotted? Comms clear?”

**Q. Can we use a Reepschnur (tag line) to retrieve the rope?**  
A. Yes, but expect snags. Keep calm and be ready to climb a little to free it if it jams.

## Wrap up with confidence

Tandem abseiling is a powerful tool to have in your climbing repertoire. It opens the door to more complex adventures, especially if you're helping an injured partner or simply want a fast way to descend. The key is practising on low-stakes terrain first. If you have access to a short cliff-such as the Kangaroo Point Cliffs in Brisbane or an easy abseil line near your home crag-run through the steps until you can do them in your sleep.

Don't forget to keep exploring higher-level rope-work so you can adapt to different scenarios. If you're keen to continue sharpening your know-how, check out other abseiling techniques that dive deeper into topics like mechanical advantage systems and self-rescue fundamentals. As you log more vertical descents, you'll discover that a calm mind-and a consistent safety routine-is the best safeguard you can bring on a climb.

Take your time, double-check every point of contact, and communicate clearly with your partner at all times. Tandem abseils may be more involved than solo descents, but they can also deepen your trust in each other. After all, there's something thrilling about sharing that final drop, together, on the same rope. It's a satisfying reminder that exploration in the mountains is rarely a solo act, and often, the best memories are forged side by side.
