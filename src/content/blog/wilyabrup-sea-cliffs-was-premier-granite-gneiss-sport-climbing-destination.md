Ask where to climb by the sea in Western Australia and one name comes up again and again: Wilyabrup. Set on the rugged coastline of the Margaret River wine region, in WA's South West, the Wilyabrup Cliffs (often called the Wilyabrup Sea Cliffs) are widely regarded as the state's premier sea-cliff climbing and abseiling destination. The rock is granite-gneiss, the drop is roughly 40 metres straight down to the Indian Ocean, and the position, perched above the swell with nothing but water below, is what makes the place unforgettable.

Wilyabrup sits within Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park, managed by the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions (DBCA, through its Parks and Wildlife Service), and it straddles the famous 123 km Cape to Cape Track that runs between Cape Naturaliste and Cape Leeuwin. It is a working climbing and abseiling venue rather than a quiet lookout: on a good spring or autumn weekend you will find ropes rigged along the clifftop and climbers moving on the wall above the water.

This guide covers what the cliffs actually are, the style and spread of climbing on offer, how to get there (including the stiles and the private-property section of the walk-in), the DBCA permit system that governs abseiling and commercial activity in the park, the coastal hazards and best seasons, and the gear-and-safety mindset a sea cliff demands. Rock climbing shares its DNA with [rap jumping](/) and abseiling. All three depend on sound anchors, careful rope-work and a calm head at height. So if the drama of Wilyabrup's ocean-edge exposure appeals but you are new to rope systems, a guided rap jumping session is a well-supervised way to get comfortable on rope before you ever rig above the sea.

## About Wilyabrup: the cliffs, the rock and the setting

The Wilyabrup Cliffs form a line of coastal cliff rising roughly 40 metres above the Indian Ocean, on the coast of the Margaret River wine region in the South West of Western Australia. They fall within Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park (the long, narrow coastal park that stretches down the Leeuwin-Naturaliste Ridge) and are managed by DBCA's Parks and Wildlife Service. The clifftop is also part of the Cape to Cape Track, the 123 km walking route that follows this stretch of coast, so climbers share the area with walkers making their way between the two capes.

The rock is granite-gneiss, a hard, metamorphosed crystalline stone that on this coast forms steep, solid sea-cliff walls. That combination of durable rock and a clean, roughly 40-metre drop straight to the ocean is a large part of why Wilyabrup has earned its reputation as WA's premier sea-cliff climbing and abseiling venue. The setting does the rest: the cliffs face the open Indian Ocean, and the sense of exposure standing at the top, or hanging on the wall above the swell, is a defining feature of climbing here rather than an incidental extra.

Because Wilyabrup is a coastal, ocean-facing cliff inside a national park, the environment is genuinely dynamic. Conditions at the base and on the lower parts of some routes are influenced by the sea (swell, spray and tide all play a part) and that shapes how, where and when people climb. It is a place that rewards checking conditions and treating the ocean as an active factor, not a backdrop.

## Climbing styles and grades at Wilyabrup

Wilyabrup offers a mix of both sport and traditional ("trad") climbing. On a sport route, the leader clips into permanently fixed protection (bolts placed in the rock) as they climb. On a trad route, the leader instead places their own removable protection (spring-loaded camming devices, wired nuts and slings) into natural cracks and constrictions, and the second climber removes each piece on the way up. Wilyabrup has ground of both kinds, which is part of what makes it such a well-rounded sea-cliff venue.

Grading follows the Ewbank system used across Australia and New Zealand, a single, open-ended number that reflects the overall difficulty, sustainedness and protection quality of a route rather than just its hardest single move. Across the cliff line, established routes span a genuinely wide band of that scale, from roughly grade 12 at the accessible, beginner-friendly end through to around grade 31 at the expert end. In other words, there is terrain here for a competent newcomer climbing with an experienced partner, and terrain that will test very strong climbers.

Because we have not independently verified individual route names, specific grades or route counts for this article, we have deliberately avoided listing them here. Reach for a current, area-specific route guide once you are planning an actual visit, and cross-check it against on-the-ground conditions. For a full explanation of the Ewbank scale, how it compares with the French and US systems, and what each grade band feels like in practice, see our companion guide, [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/), rather than have that table repeated here.

Whatever the style, a sea cliff rewards a methodical approach: sound anchor-building at the top, careful thought about where the ocean reaches on the lower sections, and honest judgement about the grade you can safely lead in an exposed, committing position above the water.

## Getting there and access: Caves Road, the track, the stiles and private property

Wilyabrup is reached from the Margaret River wine region's road network. From **Caves Road**, you turn onto **Wilyabrup Road**, which becomes a corrugated, unsealed track leading to a small car park near the coast. The unsealed section can be rough, so drive to the conditions and allow extra time; parking at the small car park is limited, particularly on busy weekends.

From the car park, the clifftop is reached on foot, a walk of about 1 km that **crosses two stiles** and passes, in part, through **private property** before reaching the coast. This access arrangement matters: the walk-in relies on the goodwill of private landholders, so treat the route with care.

- Stay on the established access route and cross at the stiles provided rather than climbing fences elsewhere.

- Leave every gate exactly as you find it. If a gate is closed, close it behind you; if it is open, leave it open.

- Keep groups tidy and quiet through the private-property section, take all rubbish out with you, and do nothing that could jeopardise ongoing access for the climbing community.

Because this is a live, managed natural area on a national-park coast, always check current conditions before you travel:

- DBCA / Parks and Wildlife Service park alerts for Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park (via exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au), for any temporary closures, track works, fire restrictions or coastal advice.

- Bureau of Meteorology forecasts for the South West, including wind, rain and (importantly for a sea cliff) swell and coastal conditions.

- Whether any part of the access route or clifftop is subject to current restrictions, and that you have whatever registration or permit is required (see the permit section below) if you are organising a group or guided activity.

## DBCA permits and the Cave and Abseil Permit System

Abseiling and climbing at Wilyabrup take place inside Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park, so they fall under DBCA's management of the park. DBCA operates a **Cave and Abseil Permit System** for Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park: under it, commercial operators and abseil instructors must be registered and permitted to run their activities within the park. In practice, that means the guided and commercial abseiling you might see at a place like Wilyabrup is expected to be delivered by operators working within DBCA's permit framework, not by anyone simply turning up with ropes and clients.

If you are an individual recreational climber, the practical takeaways are simple. Check the current DBCA requirements that apply to Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park before you go, and follow any conditions attached to access and activity in the park. If you are arranging any kind of group, course or commercial session, make sure it is run by a suitably registered and permitted operator. Requirements and processes can change, so treat DBCA's own information at exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au as the authority on what is needed at the time of your visit.

The Climbers Association of WA (CAWA) is the recognised state peak body for climbing in Western Australia and works with land managers such as DBCA on access matters affecting crags and cliffs across the state. Supporting or joining your state's climbing association is one of the most practical things a climber can do to help keep venues like Wilyabrup open and well-managed for the long term.

## Coastal hazards, whales and the best season

Wilyabrup is, above all, a coastal cliff, and that brings hazards you will not meet at an inland crag. **Swell, spray and tide** can affect some belay and base areas, and salt spray and damp leave rock slippery in places, a real consideration when you are moving around near the edge or on the lower parts of routes. The **exposure** above the open ocean is significant, both physically and psychologically. None of this makes the venue off-limits; it makes checking conditions, timing your visit to the sea state, and staying well back from unstable or wave-washed ground essential rather than optional.

There is a spectacular seasonal bonus: **humpback whales** pass along this coast roughly between June and December on their migration, and the clifftops of the Margaret River region are a fine vantage point to spot them offshore. It is a genuine highlight of climbing or walking here in the colder half of the year. Just never let whale-watching distract you from your footing near the edge.

The **best seasons for climbing at Wilyabrup are spring (September to November) and autumn (March to May)**, when temperatures are comfortable and conditions are generally at their most settled. Summer is hot, and the wider Margaret River wine region is at its busiest with holiday visitors, so the roads, car parks and coast are far more crowded. Winter can be wet and windy on this exposed coast. As always, avoid climbing on wet or spray-soaked rock. Friction drops away and the risk of a slip rises sharply.

## Gear and safety at a sea cliff

Sea-cliff climbing and abseiling carry the ordinary risks of the vertical world plus a set of coastal ones layered on top, and Wilyabrup is not a place to teach yourself the fundamentals. The consequences of a mistake above an open ocean are serious, and conditions can change with the weather and the sea. If you are new to climbing or to rope systems, the safest and fastest way to build real competency is through structured instruction from a qualified guide or mentorship from experienced climbers, ideally over multiple sessions rather than a single day.

Because Wilyabrup offers both sport and trad routes, the gear you carry depends on what you climb, but a sea-cliff kit generally includes:

- **Quickdraws**, for clipping the fixed bolts on sport routes.

- **Camming devices ("cams") and wired nuts / stoppers**, for protecting the trad routes by placing your own removable gear in natural cracks and constrictions.

- **Slings, cord and locking carabiners**, for building and equalising sound anchors at the clifftop, critical when you are rigging above the water.

- **A dynamic rope** suited to the route length, plus an abseil/belay device appropriate to your system.

As with any climbing hardware used in Australia, look for recognised certification: carabiners to EN 12275 (and/or the equivalent UIAA standard), harnesses to EN 12277, dynamic ropes to EN 892, and helmets to EN 12492. A helmet matters especially on a sea cliff, where the position is exposed and a slip or swinging fall above the ocean has serious consequences. Salt water is hard on gear, too. Rinse and inspect ropes, slings and metalware after a coastal trip, and retire anything that shows damage. Our [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/) covers these standards, and general gear and safety fundamentals, in more depth.

Finally, respect the specifics of this place: obey any DBCA conditions for Leeuwin-Naturaliste National Park, keep to the established access route and the stiles through the private-property section, leave gates as you find them, treat the swell and tide as active hazards, and pack out everything you bring in. Looking after both the environment and the access relationship is what keeps Wilyabrup open.

## Rope confidence before an ocean-edge cliff

Wilyabrup is sea-cliff climbing at its most spectacular and its most committing, with real exposure above the Indian Ocean and coastal hazards on top of the usual risks of height. Get comfortable on rope somewhere forgiving, then come back to the coast.
