## TL;DR

- **Perth's outdoor rock climbing is concentrated in the granite of the Perth Hills** (the Darling Scarp), much of it in former stone quarries a short drive east of the CBD, with Statham's Quarry, Mountain Quarry (Boya) and Churchman's Brook the best-known venues.

- **You must book in advance to climb or abseil at Statham's Quarry and Mountain Quarry.** These sites are managed by the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions (DBCA); you contact the Parks and Wildlife Mundaring office (08 9290 6100, Mon-Fri 8:30am-4pm) beforehand and are given a code to access the gate.

- **Churchman's Brook, near Kelmscott and Roleystone, is where WA climbing was pioneered from 1975** and holds some of Perth's hardest routes (including the well-known Collaboration Wall), but it sits in or near a public water-supply catchment, so access can be sensitive and must be checked before you go.

- **Climbing here is granite friction slab and bolted face climbing, graded on the open-ended Ewbank system.** Summer is hot with Total Fire Ban days and snake awareness applies in the warmer months, so the cooler months are far more comfortable.

- **Outdoor climbing carries real risk and the quarries are unforgiving of a poorly built anchor.** Beginners should seek qualified instruction rather than teach themselves, and a guided rap jumping session is a sensible, controlled way to get onto rope first.

Ask where you can climb outdoors near Perth and the answer points east, to the Darling Scarp, the long granite escarpment the locals simply call the Perth Hills. Perth is not a big natural-crag city the way some parts of the eastern states are, but it has something a little unusual: a cluster of decommissioned stone quarries whose cut granite walls have become the city's most accessible outdoor climbing, all within about forty minutes of the CBD.

These venues sit on land managed by the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions (DBCA), through the Parks and Wildlife Service, and they come with a genuinely important practical catch that trips up first-time visitors: at the main quarries you cannot simply turn up and climb. This article is one spoke in a wider series on rock climbing across Queensland and Western Australia, and Perth's Hills crags are the natural companion to the sea cliffs and boulders further south.

This guide covers what the Perth Hills granite is like, the main venues Statham's Quarry, Mountain Quarry (formerly Boya) and Churchman's Brook, the DBCA booking system and gate code you will need, the water-catchment sensitivity around Churchman's Brook, when to go, and the gear and safety mindset the quarries demand. Rock climbing shares its DNA with [rap jumping](/) and abseiling. All three depend on sound anchors, careful rope-work and a calm head at height. So if the idea of a Perth Hills quarry wall appeals but you are new to rope systems, a guided rap jumping session is a controlled, well-supervised way to get comfortable on rope before you ever tie in on real rock.

## About the Perth Hills: granite, quarries and character

Perth's outdoor climbing sits on the Darling Scarp, the weathered granite escarpment that runs along the eastern edge of the Swan Coastal Plain and forms the backdrop to the city. The rock is granite, coarse, grippy and, on the open faces, the kind of stone that rewards balance, friction and confident footwork as much as brute strength. A good deal of the climbing is not on natural cliff at all, but on the sheer, quarried walls left behind after decades of stone extraction.

That quarrying history is part of the character of the place. Statham's Quarry, on the Gooseberry Hill section of the scarp, opened as a stone quarry in 1894, and its granite went on to pave the streets of early-1900s Perth. Mountain Quarry (known for years as Boya Quarry) sits within Greenmount National Park, and like Statham's it has been repurposed from an industrial past into a recreation reserve. Today these old workings are popular climbing spots, managed by DBCA as part of the Perth Hills parks and reserves, with their smooth cut faces and blocky natural granite offering a spread of climbing.

The styles you will meet in the Hills are broadly two: granite friction and slab climbing on the lower-angle natural rock, where the challenge is trusting your feet on featured but holdless-looking stone; and steeper bolted face climbing on the quarried walls, where fixed protection has been placed and you clip your way up. Statham's Quarry in particular is often described as beginner-friendly, with a spread of grades from the accessible upward, which is part of why it is such a well-used introduction to Perth granite. We have deliberately not listed individual route names, grades or route counts for these venues. Check a current, area-specific route guide when you are planning an actual visit, and always defer to the land manager's rules on the day.

## The main venues: Statham's, Mountain Quarry and Churchman's Brook

### Statham's Quarry (Gooseberry Hill)

Statham's Quarry, on Gooseberry Hill in the Perth Hills, is one of the city's most popular and accessible climbing venues. Opened as a stone quarry in 1894 (its stone paved early-1900s Perth streets), it now offers a spread of grades from beginner-friendly upward on granite, which makes it a common first outdoor destination for Perth climbers. It is managed by DBCA, and, importantly, both rock climbing and abseiling here must be booked in advance (see the booking section below).

### Mountain Quarry (formerly Boya Quarry)

Mountain Quarry, previously known as Boya Quarry, lies within Greenmount National Park, roughly forty minutes from the Perth CBD. Its quarried granite walls carry bolted routes, and, like Statham's, it is a DBCA-managed site where climbing and abseiling require an advance booking and a gate access code. The surrounding national park setting means the usual national-park considerations apply. Stick to formed tracks and respect any signed closures or restrictions.

### Churchman's Brook (near Kelmscott / Roleystone)

Churchman's Brook, in the hills near Kelmscott and Roleystone, has a special place in the state's climbing story: it is where rock climbing in Western Australia was pioneered from 1975, and it holds some of Perth's hardest routes, including the well-known Collaboration Wall. It is a serious venue with a serious history. Crucially, Churchman's Brook sits in or near a public water-supply catchment, which makes access sensitive and subject to change. This is not a venue to assume is open. Always check the current access position and rules with the relevant authorities before you plan a trip here.

## Booking to climb: DBCA and the gate code

This is the single most important practical thing to understand about climbing the Perth Hills quarries: at Statham's Quarry and Mountain Quarry you cannot just turn up. Abseiling and rock climbing at both sites must be booked in advance, and the booking is not made through a website checkout. You contact the land manager directly.

To arrange access, phone the Parks and Wildlife Mundaring office on **08 9290 6100**, during business hours (Monday to Friday, 8:30am to 4pm). When you book, you are given a code that lets you access the gate to the site. Without that advance booking and gate code, a wasted drive to a locked gate is a very real outcome, so make the call before you commit to a plan.

Beyond the booking itself, treat these as managed conservation reserves rather than open crags:

- Confirm your booking and note the gate code before you leave home. The Mundaring office keeps standard weekday business hours, so you cannot arrange access at the last minute on a weekend.

- Check DBCA park alerts and any site-specific conditions on exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au for temporary closures, works or fire-related restrictions.

- Follow all on-site signage and any group-size or activity conditions attached to your booking.

- Leave gates as you found them and take all your gear and rubbish out with you.

## Churchman's Brook and the water catchment

Churchman's Brook deserves its own note because its access situation is different from the quarries, and more sensitive. The area is associated with a public drinking-water catchment (the Churchman Brook reservoir is part of Perth's water-supply infrastructure) and land in and around public drinking water source areas is protected accordingly. That protection can mean restrictions on public access, activities and where you are permitted to go.

What this means in practice is simple: do not assume Churchman's Brook is open to climb just because it appears in older guidebooks or trip reports. Access here can change, and it can be restricted for catchment-protection reasons that have nothing to do with the climbing itself. Before planning a visit, check the current position with the land and water managers (DBCA for the reserve and Water Corporation for the drinking-water catchment) and respect whatever the current rules are. When climbing near any water-supply catchment, keep well clear of the water body, do not pollute, and follow all signage to the letter.

## Best season, fire bans and snakes

The Perth Hills climb best in the cooler months. Perth summers are hot and dry, and open granite in full sun becomes uncomfortable, dehydrating and, on the friction slabs, genuinely harder to trust when the rock is baking. Autumn, winter and spring are far more pleasant for climbing on the exposed quarry walls and natural granite, and they are the seasons most Perth climbers favour.

Two seasonal hazards deserve real respect:

- **Total Fire Ban days and bushfire risk.** The warmer months bring high fire danger to the Perth Hills, and on Total Fire Ban days activities and access can be affected. Check the Bureau of Meteorology forecast and current fire danger ratings, and heed DBCA and local emergency-service advice before heading up, a hot, windy summer day in the Hills is not the day for it.

- **Snakes.** Snake awareness applies in the warmer months. Western Australia is home to venomous snakes that are active in the warmer seasons, and quarry ledges, boulder bases and vegetated approaches are exactly the kind of terrain to watch. Wear sturdy footwear on the approach, watch where you put hands and feet, and give any snake a wide berth.

As with any outdoor crag, avoid climbing on wet rock: granite friction climbing loses its bite when the stone is damp, and wet slab is far less forgiving of a slip.

## Gear and safety in the Perth Hills

Outdoor climbing carries meaningfully higher risk than an indoor gym, and quarry walls are unforgiving of a poorly built anchor or a mistake at the top. Bolted face climbing depends on the fixed protection being sound and on you clipping and belaying correctly; slab and friction climbing depends on trusting your feet and, where you are setting up top-ropes or abseils, on building anchors that are genuinely bombproof. None of that is something to self-teach from a video the night before.

If you are new to climbing, the safest and fastest route to real competence is qualified instruction or mentorship from experienced climbers, ideally over several sessions rather than one. Typical gear for Perth Hills climbing includes:

- **A helmet.** Important on quarried walls, where loose rock and the consequences of a fall are real.

- **A harness, belay device and locking carabiners**, and a dynamic rope suited to the routes you are climbing.

- **Quickdraws** for clipping bolted routes, plus slings and anchor material for building top-rope or abseil anchors where needed.

- **Climbing shoes with good friction**, which matter especially on granite slab, along with plenty of water and sun protection.

As with any climbing hardware used in Australia, look for recognised certification: carabiners to EN 12275 (and/or the equivalent UIAA standard), harnesses to EN 12277, dynamic ropes to EN 892, and helmets to EN 12492. Our [Rock Climbing in Australia: The Complete Guide](/rock-climbing-in-australia-the-complete-guide/) covers these standards, and general gear and safety fundamentals, in more depth. The Climbers Association of Western Australia (CAWA) is the recognised state peak body for climbing access in WA; supporting or joining your state climbing association is one of the most practical things a climber can do to help keep venues like these open for the long term.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where can you rock climb in Perth?

Perth's outdoor rock climbing is concentrated in the granite of the Perth Hills, on the Darling Scarp east of the city, much of it in former stone quarries. The best-known venues are Statham's Quarry at Gooseberry Hill, Mountain Quarry (formerly Boya Quarry) in Greenmount National Park, and Churchman's Brook near Kelmscott and Roleystone. The quarry venues are managed by the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions (DBCA).

### Do you need to book to climb at Statham's Quarry?

Yes. Rock climbing and abseiling at Statham's Quarry must be booked in advance through DBCA. You contact the Parks and Wildlife Mundaring office on 08 9290 6100, Monday to Friday between 8:30am and 4pm, and on booking you are given a code to access the gate. The same advance-booking and gate-code requirement applies at Mountain Quarry.

### What kind of climbing is there in the Perth Hills?

The Perth Hills offer granite friction and slab climbing on natural rock, plus steeper bolted face climbing on the quarried walls. Grades use the open-ended Ewbank system used across Australia and New Zealand, and Statham's Quarry in particular has a spread of grades from beginner-friendly upward. We have not listed individual route names or grades here; check a current route guide when planning a visit.

### When is the best time to go climbing in Perth?

The cooler months. Perth summers are hot and the open granite becomes uncomfortable in full sun, so autumn, winter and spring are far more pleasant for climbing. The warmer months also bring high bushfire danger, with Total Fire Ban days that can affect access, and snakes are active, so check the Bureau of Meteorology forecast and fire danger ratings before you go.

### Is Perth Hills climbing suitable for beginners?

Some venues, such as Statham's Quarry, have beginner-friendly grades, but outdoor climbing still carries real risk and the quarry walls are unforgiving of mistakes. Beginners should learn through qualified instruction or mentorship from experienced climbers rather than teaching themselves. A guided rap jumping session is a controlled way to get comfortable on rope before climbing outdoors.

## Before the gate code and the quarry wall

The Perth Hills reward climbers who can build a solid anchor, trust their feet on granite and stay calm on a quarry wall. Those are skills worth learning properly rather than improvising. A guided [rap jumping](/) or abseiling session with a qualified operator uses certified equipment and has every system checked by an instructor. It is a straightforward first step, and it does not require booking a gate code weeks ahead.
